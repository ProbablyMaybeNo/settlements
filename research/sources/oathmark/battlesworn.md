INTRODUCTION
The story of those standards tall, shall strike an echo through the ages. 
Of a thousand times, a thousand brothers, all battlesworn but doomed to fall.
Fragment, The Lay of the Marches
Welcome to Battlesworn, the first supplement for Oathmark: Battles of the Lost 
Age. Within these pages, players can discover a host of new material to add more 
flavour to their kingdoms and armies and tactical variety to their battles. The new 
rules presented in this book fall broadly into three categories. 
The first and shortest covers battle honours and is likely to be the most 
immediately interesting to players. Essentially, it allows units that perform well 
in a specific battle to become unique. Players record these units on a Battle 
Honours Sheet and track the units’ actions from battle to battle. As they continue 
to fight battles and accrue more battle honours, these units become more 
effective; however, should they suffer defeat and destruction during a battle, it is 
possible to lose the bonds and experience that make the unit special. These rules 
allow players to track the progress of their kingdom as a whole, as well as focus 
on some of the storied units that march under its banners.
This book’s second main theme is water; specifically, rivers and lakes. There 
are new rules for using boats during a battle – for amphibious assaults and as 
firing platforms – and for fighting skirmishes on the water. There are also several 
new unit types that go along with these rules. These include the boats themselves, 
the crews required to man them, and a unit of engineers for each race. Engineer 
units are invaluable to an army; they can build (or destroy) bridges to cross rivers, 
build temporary fortifications to use as cover for missile troops, and hastily 
construct a defensive position.
Finally, this book presents three different military expeditions. Essentially, these 
are narrative mini-campaigns that sit within the campaign framework presented 
in the main rulebook. Each military expedition contains two or more closely 
linked scenarios, the outcomes of which have a direct effect on the next battle 
and give the winner some advantage in position, troop numbers, morale, etc. 
These expeditions may also offer kingdoms a specific or unique reward, such as 
capturing a unique territory. Most of the scenarios in the military expeditions 
feature either a river or lake, to give players an excuse to test out the new rules.

BATTLE HONOURS
Most units, with enough drilling and practice, can be taught to manoeuvre and 
fight as one; however, it is not until units face the horrors of battle that their true 
strengths and weaknesses are revealed. Some break as soon as the situation turns 
against them. Others fight to the last man, dying for a hopeless cause, in order to 
stay true to their oathmark. Unfortunately, even the greatest generals can rarely 
determine which units are which before battle.
Over time, though, certain units acquire reputations as tough, determined 
fighters. These are units that stand the test of battle – the ones you can count on 
when the fighting is at its height. Traditionally, such units are awarded battle 
honours, usually represented by a badge, medal, or some other token worn on the 
warriors’ armour. If the unit has a standard, the honour is often represented there 
by the name of the battle during which the unit won the honour. 
These rules allow players to award battle honours to units that fight in their 
battles. When a player awards a unit an honour, that unit becomes a unique,

fixed, part of the player’s kingdom. Such units receive a limited number of 
rerolls, equal to their number of battle honours, which they can use throughout 
a battle. Due to this increased ability, these units also cost more points. As these 
units participate in additional battles, they may add additional battle honours or, 
if fate turns against them, they may be destroyed. 
To keep track of these unique units, players should copy the Battle Honours 
Sheet from the back of this book or download a copy from the Osprey Games 
Resources Page at www.ospreygames.co.uk. The sheet includes space to record 
all the necessary information for these units and should be kept with a player’s 
Kingdom Sheet.
Awarding Battle Honours
At the end of any major battle, each player may award one battle honour to a 
unit in their army that performed well during that battle. The exact definition of 
a major battle is left to the players, but it generally includes any battle in which 
each player has at least 1,500 points worth of figures. When playing a scenario, 
it includes but is not necessarily limited to any battle in which the winning 
player has the option to add new territory to their kingdom. 
To award a battle honour, the player simply selects one unit that participated 
in the battle that meets all the following requirements:
•	 The unit had at least one figure remaining on the table at the end of the 
battle.
•	 The unit started the battle with at least 9 figures on 25 x 25mm bases or 4 
figures on 25 x 50mm bases.
•	 The unit either eliminated or caused one or more enemy units to become 
Broken during the battle, or it scored points at the end of the battle that 
counted towards a player’s victory. (For example, if a scenario’s victory 
conditions said, “award 2 points for every unit within 6” of an objective,” this 
unit qualifies for a battle honour. This does not include scenarios where a 
player simply counts their units’ point values to determine victory).
•	 The unit does not have the special abilities Unthinking or Spellcaster Control 
(Unthinking is presented in a later book).
•	 The unit does not contain the words militia or slave in its name.
•	 The unit currently has fewer than three battle honours.

Recording Battle Honours
As soon as you award a battle honour to a unit, add that unit to your Battle 
Honours Sheet. Note, however, that when you award a battle honour, you only 
award it to the unit itself, not any character figures that fought with the unit 
during the battle. For example, if your army had a unit of 19 elf spearmen with 
a Champion, record only the 19 elf spearmen on the Battle Honours Sheet. 
Fill in all the entries for a unit the same way you would on an Army Sheet, 
with two changes. First, under special abilities, add Battle Honours (1). If the unit 
is already on the Battle Honours Sheet, simply increase their battle honours by 
one to Battle Honours (2) or Battle Honours (3). Remember, no unit may have 
more than three battle honours. Second, increase the unit’s cost by 10%, rounding 
to the nearest full point. Do this each time the unit gains a new battle honour, 
taking 10% of the current cost, including increases for previous battle honours. 
For example, a unit of 19 elf spearmen costs 418 points. If you award this unit a 
battle honour, its cost increases to 460 points; if you award it a second battle 
honour, its cost increases to 506 points; and if you award it a third battle honour, 
its cost increases to 557 points. 
Although it is not necessary, you may want to list the name of the battle in 
which the unit gained its battle honour in the notes. This may be the scenario 
name or a name the players give to the battle. Not only does this give the unit a 
real history, it also helps players remember some of their great past games.

RECRUITING UNITS WITH BATTLE 
HONOURS
When you muster an army, you may include one unit from your Battle Honours 
Sheet. To do so, simply pay the point cost, as usual. This unit is still subject to all 
the rules concerning the territory in a kingdom. Thus, they still count toward the 
maximum number of units available from a territory and, if drawn from an 
occupied territory, they still suffer the activation penalty. They also count toward 
the maximum of 4 units of any one type. 
You must field the unit exactly as listed on the Battle Honours Sheet; you may 
not add or subtract figures from the unit. There are a few exceptions to this rule. 
The first is that if the unit is one figure short of completing a rank, you may add 
one figure to the unit at the usual cost for a figure of that type. So, a unit of 19 
elf spearmen could add one more elf spearman, at the usual cost of 22 points, to 
increase the number of figures in the unit to 20. Or, you may choose to add a 
character figure to the unit. However, if the unit has only 18 spearmen, you may 
only add a character figure. 
The second exception is that if the unit contains 5, 10, 15, or 20 figures, you 
may drop one figure from the unit and replace it with a character for the 
upcoming battle. In this case, simply subtract the cost of a single figure from the 
unit. So, if the unit of elf spearmen originally had 20 members, you could drop 
it down to 19 and gain a refund of 22 points, but only if you then place a 
character within the unit. 
Astute players may note that a single elf spearman that is part of a unit with 
battle honours is worth slightly more than 22 points, but that is the penalty you 
pay for messing with a unit that has battle experience.
MULTIPLE UNITS WITH BATTLE HONOURS
These rules allow each army to include only one unit with battle honours, though a kingdom can 
theoretically possess an infinite number. This is merely to keep the game simple – players can 
typically track one unit with battle honours per side without slowing down the game or forgetting 
about them. That said, there is mechanically nothing to prevent players from using multiple units 
with battle honours in the same army, provided they have enough points available to include them. 
Once players are comfortable enough with these rules, they may decide to try a game in which 
several units per side have battle honours. This is especially appropriate for games that use high 
point values.
Players should consider allowing more than one unit with battle honours when playing a 
campaign in which a player’s capital city comes under attack. In such instances, it makes sense 
that a kingdom’s best units would rally to its defence. As with all Oathmark rules, use everything 
presented as a guideline to have fun during a game; modify or change any rules you believe would 
make your games more enjoyable.

Spending Battle Honours
During a game, a unit with battle honours may spend a battle honour to reroll a 
single die from an activation, morale test, combat, or shooting action roll. Players 
may not spend battle honours to reroll the Champion Die. If a player chooses to 
reroll a die, they must keep the second roll, even if the result is worse.
A unit may only use one battle honour per roll. Thus, a player may only reroll 
one die as part of the rolls mentioned above. Beyond that, there is no limit to 
how a unit may spend its battle honours; a unit with three battle honours could 
theoretically spend them all during the same activation, provided they are for 
separate rolls. 
For example, if our unit of elf spearmen with Battle Honours (3) activates, it 
could reroll a die from its activation roll, its combat roll, and its morale test, all 
in the same turn. Alternatively, it could reroll a die from its activation roll on 
three consecutive turns. 
Though rare, if two units that have battle honours are in combat with one 
another, both could potentially use battle honours. In such cases, the player that 
has priority that turn must decide first whether they wish to spend a battle honour. 
The other player may wait to see their opponent’s choice – and the result of any 
reroll – before deciding whether their unit also wishes to spend a battle honour.

When spending battle honours, the key is to remember that they are limited 
and precious commodities that you should save for moments when they can have 
the greatest impact. While players may be tempted to reroll a Combat Die every 
time a unit that has battle honours gets into a fight, they should consider the 
potential consequences of making that reroll. If their unit already won the fight, 
does inflicting one more casualty make a huge difference? Is failing an activation 
roll at this point in the game really that important? When in doubt, ask yourself, 
“would I rather spend this battle honour now, or save it for when my unit fails a 
morale test that could potentially Break it and remove it from the table?”
Battle honours not only provide the chance to cause more casualties in a fight 
(though there are times when that might be your best option), they are crucial 
elements of tactical flexibility that can turn the tide of a battle when used at just 
the right moment.
Regaining Battle Honours
A unit that has battle honours, participates in a battle, and has at least one figure 
in the unit remaining on the table at the end of that battle regains full battle 
honours for the next game. The unit could even potentially be awarded a new 
battle honour. 
In other words, if a unit with Battle Honours (2) fights in a battle and has at 
least one figure remaining on the table at the end of that battle, it starts the next 
game with Battle Honours (2) – or Battle Honours (3), if it gained a new battle 
honour – no matter how many battle honours the unit did or did not spend 
during the battle. A unit cannot save battle honours from one battle to the next.

PLANTING AN OATHMARK
Many of the soldiers in the Marches carry their own personal oathmarks. These may be written on scraps 
of paper, sewn into clothing, painted on a shield, or even tattooed somewhere on their body. Many orcs, 
for example, like to carve their oathmarks into a bone from a fallen foe and then wear it as a necklace or 
dangle it from their belt. Along with these personal declarations, an army sometimes carries its own 
oathmark – a large, physical declaration of their oath to king, country, or god. These have as many 
different forms as the personal oathmarks: a large statue carried by a group of slaves, a vast banner 
carried by an ogre, or a stone tablet held aloft by strange magics. Some elf kingdoms are famous for 
engraving their oathmarks onto large silver bells, which teams of horses pull in purpose-built carts. Of 
course, since it is impractical to take many of these items on campaign, they are often reserved for the 
largest armies or the most desperate of battles.
 Within a kingdom’s borders, more permanent oathmarks are often placed at strategic, defensive 
points, such as hills, river crossings, or valley roads. These not only serve as declarations of loyalty, 
but also as notices to travellers and rallying points during an invasion. Rulers can often predict the 
most likely lines of invasion and may place oathmarks along these avenues to bolster the morale of 
their troops. Statues, standing stones, and columns are the most common; but you may also see small 
shrines, magical fountains, and large stakes decorated with skulls. There are no overarching in-game 
rules for how to display oathmarks;  players may want to experiment with their favourite techniques and 
incorporate their kingdom’s culture. 
The optional rule for planting oathmarks during a game is included to give players a reason and 
opportunity to craft their own oathmarks for their army. So, if you have always wanted to model a statue 
in a cart pushed by a group of goblins, now is your chance. Alternatively, if you like the rules but are not 
that big into the hobby side of things, you can make a simple, paper banner or paint a quick symbol on 
a rock. If you are playing the defender in a Deep Strike, Invasion, or Border Strike, you might want to 
consider using a more permanent-looking oathmark, such as a standing stone or large statue.
Planting oathmarks might not be appropriate or useful in some scenarios, but players are free to make 
that decision. Though it might not make sense at first to plant an oathmark on a table that is made up of 
mainly water, perhaps the army built a raft to hold it….
The Rules
After each army deploys all its units, but before rolling for initiative the first turn, each player has the 
option to plant an oathmark. Starting with the player that places the last unit on the table, each player 
declares whether they are planting an oathmark. If they choose to, they may place it anywhere they 
may legally place a starting unit. A planted oathmark may never move during a battle.
During the game, any unit within 6” of their army’s oathmark gains a +1 to all morale tests. Units 
that already have Courage (X) or the special abilities Enormous or Monster do not receive this benefit.
If an enemy unit ever moves into contact with an oathmark, that unit’s activation ends immediately 
and the oathmark is automatically destroyed and removed from the table. Any units belonging to the 
army that planted the oathmark that are within 6” of the oathmark when it is destroyed must make a 
morale test for Cascading Panic as though a friendly unit had just Broken – possibly forcing additional 
morale tests on units farther away as described in the rules for Cascading Panic in the main rulebook.

ENGINEERS AND  
RIVERINE CRAFT
This chapter introduces two new unit types you can add to your army. Both units 
have specialized roles; thus, they are not useful in every game or scenario. 
However, there are times when these units can give canny commanders a 
significant edge on the battlefield. Many of the scenarios presented in this book 
are designed with these units in mind.
Engineers
Engineers are small infantry units that specialize in the construction and 
destruction of battlefield fortifications, temporary bridges, and roads. Although 
a lot of their work is done before a battle to help move an army through difficult 
terrain, they can also play a vital role during a fight. 
All the major races use engineer units, though some excel at it more than 
others. To include an engineer unit in your army, you need the appropriate 
territory in your kingdom. This is noted in the figure lists below. Like artillery 
units, engineer units have a fixed number of figures: either 5 or 10. This is listed 
in the notes for the unit and is the maximum number of figures a unit of this 
type may ever include. If you want more engineers in your army, simply recruit 
more units. It is possible to combine two units that both suffer casualties, 
following the rules for combining units (see Oathmark page 66), but you may 
not increase a unit above its normal starting number in this way. 
Engineer units activate, move, fight, and perform all other actions in the same 
manner as other units, but also have several additional actions that they may take. 
If an engineer unit passes its activation roll, it may forgo all its actions that turn 
to build a temporary fortification. Place this fortification directly in front of the 
unit. It should be approximately half an inch high with a length equal to the unit. 
(Thus, a unit that contains three dwarf engineers would produce a 75mm long

fortification – equal to the width of the three figures’ bases). This fortification 
counts as light cover and as defensive fortifications for any unit that attacks over 
them. The fortification remains until the end of the game or until destroyed by 
another engineer unit. Engineer units never suffer a movement penalty for 
moving over temporary fortifications – whether they are their own, those built 
by another engineer unit, or those on the table at the start of the game.
If an engineer unit is in contact with a fortification – temporary or permanent 
– and passes an activation roll, it may spend an action to destroy the portion of the 
fortification with which it is in contact. So, a unit of five dwarf engineers standing 
flat against a wall could destroy 125mm of it. Engineer units may not destroy 
wooded areas, as the time lapse during a battle does not allow for this. If players 
use engineer units, it is worth spending a moment before each game to discuss 
which terrain pieces they could potentially destroy.
If an engineer unit moves into contact with a terrain piece, it can make a free 
wheel manoeuvre, provided the manoeuvre moves as much of the unit into contact 
with the terrain piece as possible.
If an engineer unit moves across a river, the unit may split, leaving one or more 
figures at the point where the unit entered the river. The rest of the figures may 
continue their movement, but must stop as soon as they reach the far side of the 
river. All these figures still count as part of a single unit and continue to make 
activation rolls and morale tests as part of this unit; however, if either sub-unit is 
attacked, use only the figures in that sub-unit to determine Combat Dice and 
complete ranks. Additionally, a sub-unit may not suffer more casualties than the 
number of figures in the sub-unit being attacked. The unit can re-form simply 
by taking a move action that moves one sub-unit back into contact with the 
other. If this happens, the unit may reorganize around the sub-unit that did not 
move, so all the figures are in a legal formation, facing the same direction. This 
is a free action.
If an engineer unit has figures on both sides of a river and it passes its activation 
roll, it may forgo all its actions that activation to construct a temporary bridge. 
Immediately place a marker to represent this bridge. Any unit, except those with 
the special ability Enormous, may now use this bridge to cross the river. The actual 
size of the unit and the bridge do not matter; assume the bridge is large enough to 
accommodate any unit.
If an engineer unit passes its activation roll while in contact with either end of 
a temporary bridge, and there is no unit on that bridge and no enemy unit within 
6”, then the engineer unit may forgo all its actions during that activation to destroy 
the temporary bridge. Note that this rule does not apply to permanent bridges that 
are part of the scenario set-up. Such bridges are built to a much higher standard 
and take more time to destroy than is generally available during a battle.

Riverine Craft
Numerous waterways criss-cross the lands of the Marches, from small brooks, 
streams, and ghylls, to mighty rivers. Where the rivers are large and calm enough 
to handle boats, they are the central highways of the land – the quickest and 
easiest method of transportation, especially for trade goods. Because many 
kingdoms already use these rivers for mercantile purposes, they also tend to rely 
on them in times of war to transport troops and materials. In general, boats tend 
to play a supporting role in the fighting, either as transportation or the occasional 
floating, firing platform for artillery or bow-armed soldiers. Although rare, a few 
battles take place fully on rivers, where troops on boats clash on the water. Most 
generals avoid such engagements, however, as drowning deaths greatly increase 
the casualty rates and make such battles extremely costly.
This section presents rules for adding river boats to your army and for using 
those boats during a game to provide a river-based shooting platform, allowing 
your army to launch an amphibious assault or fight enemy riverine units.
ADDING RIVERINE CRAFT TO AN ARMY
There are numerous types and varieties of riverine craft; the exact look of a boat 
or barge can vary greatly, depending on the craftsmen that built it and the 
kingdom which employs it. Despite any cosmetic differences, all riverine craft in 
Oathmark are designated as one of two types: Small Boats and Large Boats.
You may add boats to any army that has the appropriate territories in its 
kingdom, but you can only use them in a scenario that contains a river large 
enough for boats or a lake. This is either stated in the specific scenario or players 
can agree before a game – preferably before each player chooses their army! The 
boats themselves do not cost any points to add to an army; however, a player 
must purchase a crew to man them to employ them in a game. Small Boats have 
a crew of 3, while Large Boats have a crew of 5. Any unit that contains the word 
‘soldier’ or ‘militia’ in its name may crew a boat, as can Elf Pathguards. 
A player can only purchase crew of the race that matches their Capital City 
or the territory they used to purchase the boat. The kingdom need not contain 
the actual territory that usually produces that type of figure. For example, a 
kingdom with an Orc Capital City and a Lumber Yard that wants to add a Small 
Boat to its army may either crew it with 3 orc soldiers (39 points) or 3 dwarf 
militia (33 points). Neither boats nor crews are subject to the usual 4 per unit 
type per army maximum. An army may contain as many boats and crews of a 
single type as they can afford.

FIGURES ON BOATS
You must place all of a boat’s crew on one boat and they may never leave their 
boat during a game. It does not matter where in the boat you place the crew, nor 
is it important to place them in any kind of formation, or even together. It is fine 
to scatter them about, as they work at their different stations on the boat.
Apart from the crew, each boat may hold either one or two units, depending 
on the size of the boat. It does not matter how many figures are in the unit. So, 
a Large Boat could hold two units of 20 goblin archers, or two goblin spellcasters 
(units-of-one), or one of each. Units should be placed in their usual formations 
while on a boat.
Figures mounted on horses or wolves and those with the special abilities 
Enormous or Monster may not ride on boats.
Moving Boats
Boats and their crew count as a single unit within an army and activate following 
the same rules. However, whenever a boat activates, it must move with the river 
before it takes any other actions.
All rivers in Oathmark have a river velocity, which is listed in the scenario or 
decided by the players before a game. A river velocity consists of a direction and 
a number of inches, which tells the players the direction and distance a boat 
must move when it is activated, before it takes any other actions. 
MODELLING BOATS
A boat’s exact size and shape does not have a major impact on the rules, and players should feel 
free to model their boats to suit the units that are likely to ride in them. Thus, if you know that your 
boat is going to contain a catapult and a unit of 20 archers, you know you need a large boat. If you 
are just planning to put a spellcaster in it, a small boat will do.
The easiest way to create a boat is to take the units you want to use in that boat, place them on 
a piece of cardboard, and draw the outline of a boat around them. Remember to leave some extra 
room to fit the boat’s crew. You can then cut out this outline and have a footprint of the deck. If you 
are in a hurry, or not much into modelling, this cardboard cut-out can serve as your boat during the 
game. If you are so inclined, however, you can use the cut-out as the template for the deck and 
build a more elaborate boat from cardboard, foam board, or balsa wood – and even add railings 
and raised decks at the front and back for a small unit or for crew. Again, the rules for boats are 
open; they should not get in the way of adding extra detail. It is worth noting though, that most 
riverine craft are more like barges than ships and tend to be flat-bottomed and lie low in the water.

SHOOTING FROM BOATS
When using boats during a battle, the preferred tactic for most kingdoms is to use 
them as a protected shooting platform. Archers or artillery can fire on enemy units 
from the deck of a boat without the risk of being attacked in hand-to-hand combat 
(unless the enemy units have the special abilities Waterwalk or Flying, of course). 
Units on boats follow all the usual rules for missile attacks, except that they 
apply an additional +1 to the Target Number for firing from an unstable platform. 
See the addition to the Shooting Modifier Table below.
RANDOM RIVER VELOCITY
When players create their own scenarios that include a river, they may wish to roll randomly for 
the river velocity. Some scenarios also call for random river velocity. In such cases, select a point 
at which the river intersects the edge of the table and roll a die. On a 1–5, the river flows toward 
this table edge. On a 6–10, it flows away from this table edge. Roll the die again, divide the result 
in half, and round down. This number, between 0 and 5 is the river velocity in inches. 
Players should roll for random river velocity before they place any units on the table.

SHOOTING AT BOATS
In game terms, it is impossible to shoot directly at a boat; few of the weapons 
employed in the game could damage one, anyway. Instead, a unit must target an 
opposing unit on the boat. A unit may only target the crew on a boat if there are 
no other units on the boat. 
Units on a boat never count as having cover; however, there is an additional 
+1 modifier when shooting at a unit on a boat, to account for the small bits of 
cover provided by the sides of a boat and the greater difficulty to hit targets that 
are bobbing in the water.
If shooting attacks kill all the crew on a boat, the boat is destroyed and is

EMBARKING AND DISEMBARKING
If a boat is in contact with land, units on board may move off the boat onto land 
following the normal rules for movement. Units may move through the crew and 
over small bits of water (up to an inch), so long as they end their activation fully 
on land. 
A unit may move off a boat and directly into combat; however, in this case, 
the moving unit does not receive a bonus for extra ranks, due to the confusion 
of disembarking. If an enemy unit is close enough to the boat that the unit 
cannot fully disembark before it makes contact, it can still make the move. Leave 
the unit partially on the boat and partially in the water and fight the combat. If 
the disembarking unit wins, it must push the enemy back 6” and then move fully 
onto land. If the disembarking unit loses, it is pushed back fully onto the boat, 
and suffers additional damage as though it had lost a fight on water.
A unit may move onto a friendly boat simply by making a move action that 
takes the unit onto the boat. At the end of this move action, the unit gets a free 
manoeuvre to adjust the unit so it fits on the boat. This unit’s activation ends 
automatically after this manoeuvre, even if it had actions remaining.
If a unit moves into contact with an enemy boat, it counts as moving into 
combat with one of the units on the boat. The boat’s owner may choose which 
unit fights, but may only choose the crew if it is the only unit remaining on the 
boat. Fight this battle as usual; however, if a unit on a boat loses a fight, it suffers 
additional damage as though it had lost a fight on water. If the boat has no units 
other than the crew on board, immediately place the crew in formation at the 
edge of the boat so it directly opposes the attacking unit. Fight the combat, as

FIGHTING ON THE WATER
If a boat moves into contact with an enemy boat, either side may choose to initiate 
a round of combat. This is not required; if neither side wishes to fight, immediately 
move the boats one inch apart. However, if one side or the other does wish to fight, 
both sides must select one unit in the boat to fight. It does not matter what 
position this unit has on the boat, or if it is the closest unit to the enemy boat. The 
only limitation is that you may only select the crew to fight if there are no other 
units on the boat. All units that fight while on a boat count as fighting to their front 
arc, no matter the relative positions of the boats or the units in them.
Once the players select their units, the units fight a round of combat – as usual 
– with two special modifiers. First, due to the increased danger and difficulty of 
fighting on boats, all units suffer -1 to their Target Number. Second, because it is 
nearly impossible to build up momentum when attacking an enemy on a boat, 
units in the fight may neither apply bonuses for the special ability Charge nor 
claim rerolls for the special ability Wild Charge.
Once the combat results are determined and morale tests are made, the unit 
that lost the fight immediately suffers an additional point of damage. This 
represents being pushed back into the water. This point of damage does not prompt 
any further morale tests. 
After a fight, separate the boats by one inch. The boats must move back into 
contact to fight again, at which time the players may select the same units to fight, 
or different units if any other legal units are available. 
If a boat moves into contact with an enemy boat and fights a round of combat, 
that boat’s activation ends immediately – even if it had actions remaining. Because 
boats must move with the river, this may mean a boat drifts into another boat, 
fights a round of combat, and its activation ends before it ever takes an action.
LAKES
Aside from rivers, most bodies of water on a wargaming table are – at best – ponds, unworthy of 
boats. However, if you are playing the game on a large table, or if a significant portion of the table 
contains water, it is possible to have a lake, or at least a partial lake. Lakes follow all the rules 
presented here, with one major exception – they do not have river velocity. Just ignore the move 
with the river rule when playing with boats on lakes. 
In cases where a river is attached to the lake, the river does have a river velocity, and a boat is 
subject to that velocity as soon as any part of it is in the river section.

MILITARY EXPEDITIONS
The Oathmark main rulebook contains a detailed campaign system for fighting 
battles in the Marches and determining the effects those battles have on a 
kingdom. This book contains three additional military expeditions you can use 
within the context of that campaign system. Each expedition contains two or 
more interconnected battle scenarios that are designed to be played in order by 
the same two players. The outcome of each battle has a direct effect on the next, 
and they all combine to tell the story of one chapter in a kingdom’s military 
history. Each expedition also offers the potential to earn special rewards that are 
not generally available. For example, the first expedition tells the story of battles 
fought by two different kingdoms to claim a ruined temple complex. The winner 
of the final battle can claim the temple and add it to their kingdom as a unique 
territory. 
When playing these expeditions, it is not necessary to roll for the battle type 
before the game. Each scenario dictates the attacker and defender and what the 
battle results mean within the campaign. While each expedition calls for specific 
terrain and table set-up, players may modify these to match their available space 
and terrain collection. Provided the table appears similar to the description given 
in the set-up, the game should not need any rules modifications. 
Even though all battles in one military expedition take place during a short 
time span and away from the kingdom, players may select a new army for each 
game. This represents units that are part of the army, but may not always be in 
position to take part in every battle.
Because all these expeditions take place in areas near water, new Strange and 
Catastrophic Events Tables are presented at the end of the chapter for use during 
these battles (see page 61).

AMPHIBIOUS ASSAULTS
After reading the rules for boats and seeing some of the scenarios presented in this chapter, 
players may want to play a game that features a major, opposed amphibious landing – sort of a 
fantasy version of D-Day with a lot of dug-in catapults and ballista on a hill that fire down on boats 
as they approach the shore. If so, I say, “go for it!” I did not include such a scenario in this book 
because of the preparation work likely needed for such a game. The players would need enough 
boats to hold an entire army, a table large enough to hold all those boats, and enough room 
remaining to depict both the sea and the sand with a bit of space to manoeuvre. 
Further, attempting to balance such a scenario is extremely tricky. The defender has a huge 
advantage. Not only do they benefit from the natural cover and optimal firing position, but they are 
also firing from steady ground, unlike their opponents in boats. Even worse, once the attackers hit 
land, they are likely to meet immediate opposition and run the very real danger of losing additional 
men and being driven back into the sea. This is, of course, all part of the fun; but you need to 
consider such things when setting up the scenario. 
Still, even with all these advantages, the defender does not have machine guns or high-explosive 
artillery, so the imbalance between attacker and defender is not as bad as D-Day. However, the 
attacker will likely need 25% to 75% more points-worth of forces than the defender. When playing 
such a game, ignore the actual cost of the boats; they do not provide any benefit to the attacker in 
terms of military strength, they are merely a means of conveyance (or an unstable firing platform). 
If you want to work such a scenario into your campaign, it fits well as a follow-up to Military 
Expedition 2. Perhaps six months pass, and the kingdom that lost the expedition went home, 
regrouped, and organized a last major attempt to take the island and its magical flowers. For this 
scenario, the winner of Military Expedition 2 plays the defender and has had time to build some 
semi-permanent fortifications and firing positions on the island. 
If the attacker wins the scenario, they capture the island and the unique territory changes hands 
from one kingdom to the other. If this does happen, though, it is only fair for the winner to allow 
the loser their own chance to launch a major amphibious assault and attempt to retake it!

New Spells
The new spells presented here all relate to water; there must be a water-based 
terrain feature on the table in order to use them. Apart from that limitation, you 
may select and cast these spells following the rules presented in the Oathmark 
main rulebook. 
GENERAL SPELLS
Calm the Waters (CN4)
Immediately reduce river velocity to 0”. At the end of each turn, roll a die: on a 
1 or 2, the river returns to its normal velocity.
Enrage the Waters (CN6)
Modify river velocity by 4”. The spellcaster may choose whether this increases 
the river velocity by 4” in the direction it is currently flowing, or subtracts 4” 
from the current direction of flow – possibly causing the river to flow in the 
opposite direction. For example, if the spell is cast on a river with a river velocity 
of 2” to the north, the spellcaster may cause this to become 2” to the south. At 
the end of each turn, roll a die: on a 1 the river returns to its normal velocity.
Summon Water Elemental (CN9)
Place a water elemental on the table within 6” and line of sight of the caster. This 
water elemental counts as a member of the spellcaster’s army. A spellcaster may 
not cast this spell while a water elemental they previously summoned remains 
on the table. Additionally, while the water elemental is on the table, the spellcaster 
suffers -1 to all casting dice they roll to cast other spells.

Unique Territories
Unique territories are new types of territories that you may only add to a kingdom 
under specific circumstances. Most unique territories are a prize for winning a 
military expedition, and are noted in the scenario outcome. 
The only other way to claim a unique territory is to take it from a kingdom 
that has it. This is a two-step process. Whenever a kingdom wins a battle and may 
occupy a territory in a rival kingdom, it may choose to occupy a unique territory. 
While occupied, neither kingdom may use any of the special benefits offered by 
a unique territory. If the kingdom occupying the unique territory wins another 
battle against the same kingdom, which would allow them to occupy another 
territory, they may claim the unique territory, instead. Remove the unique 
territory from the Kingdom Sheet of the original owner and add it to any open 
space in Region 5 of the victorious kingdom’s Kingdom Sheet. If this kingdom 
has no open spaces in Region 5, they may replace one of their current territories 
with the unique territory. This kingdom now gains all the benefits of the unique 
territory.
A unique territory can pass back and forth between kingdoms in this way 
several times during a campaign. Unsurprisingly, each unique territory is, indeed, 
unique. Only one kingdom in a given campaign may contain each type of unique 
territory.

New Special Abilities
AQUATIC
This creature lives in water and never suffers penalties of any kind for being in 
water. This creature never voluntarily moves onto land. If it is ever on land, its 
Move stat is immediately reduced to 1 and it suffers -2 to its Fight stat (to a 
minimum of 0). Additionally, every time an Aquatic creature on land fails an 
activation roll, it becomes either Disordered or Broken, as though it failed a 
morale test. A Disordered aquatic creature on land remains Disordered even if 
it passes an activation roll. It may only recover from being Disordered when it is 
once again in the water.
SPELLCASTER CONTROL
This figure may only join an army that contains a Spellcaster. Further, if at any 
point the army has no Spellcaster on the table, immediately remove all figures 
in that army with this ability and count them as casualties.
