# Rule of Carnage Skirmish Game Design Reference

A tabletop skirmish game needs an experience that players want to repeat. Its resolution systems, activations, movement, objectives, roster and campaign should support that experience, and its rulebook must let people produce it without the designer in the room.

This reference brings together design arguments and examples from the six selected Rule of Carnage playlists: **The Basics, Getting Started, Advanced Design, Playtesting, Other Mechanics, and Cursed Problems**. The collection contains **83 distinct videos**, approximately **47 hours 35 minutes**, and **512,063 words of captured captions**. The playlists contain 96 entries because some videos repeat. [[Rule of Carnage Episode Index]] records every source and capture method.

The advice is contextual. A technique that supports tense competition may undermine a chaotic narrative game; a harsh survival game may intentionally accept experiences another design should avoid. The hosts' examples explain design choices rather than supply complete or current rules for the games mentioned. Caption errors can affect names and terminology, so important passages have direct video links.

This is external design research. It does not adopt mechanics into [[Archive/Settlements/2026-10-24 - Retired organization/Rules System/Second Draft/Full Rules System v2|Settlements Draft 2]] or decide its intentionally unfinished systems.

For the detailed findings, examples and qualifications from each video, use [[Rule of Carnage Episode Notes]]. This guide combines recurring arguments by topic; the companion retains episode-level detail. Practical applications and the worksheet below are editorial synthesis. Introductions, promotions and unrelated conversation are outside the design reference's scope.

## Start with the experience and the repeated decisions

Before choosing a die, activation system or weapon profile, describe the experience you want. What should a player anticipate, decide, discover and feel? A setting or collection of miniatures can provide a reason to care, but the ordinary turn still needs to do useful work.

The **core loop** is the activity the player repeatedly returns to. In a familiar combat loop, the player selects a priority target, moves into position, attacks, resolves the result and selects another target. The hosts ask whether the particular game offers enough interest within that loop to make the next turn attractive. They do not insist that every game must replace it: narrative, hobby appeal, unusually smooth play or the satisfaction of fielding large armies can be deliberate reasons to use a familiar structure. [Core Loop, 0:45–4:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtqS8bLGJ3c&t=45s), [Core Loop, 17:10–23:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtqS8bLGJ3c&t=1030s)

Several different kinds of loop emerge from their examples:

| Approach | What repeatedly engages the player | Design question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Predict and reveal | Anticipate where pieces will end up, commit to a plan, then discover what happened | Can a player learn from a mistaken prediction? |
| Allocate resources | Decide how a hand, dice allocation or order resource will shape this round | Do the resources create a coherent plan across individual activations? |
| Manipulate physical objects | Attempt a tangible task and experience its success or failure | Is the physical skill appropriate for the audience and theme? |
| Prioritize and execute | Choose where to concentrate force and carry out the plan efficiently | Is executing the plan satisfying, and does the board create fresh decisions? |

These are interpretations of examples including X-Wing, Malifaux, Saga, Gaslands and Space Gits, rather than complete descriptions of their rules. [Core Loop, 4:20–11:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtqS8bLGJ3c&t=260s)

**Feedback matters as much as the decision.** When a player chooses a movement template, tries it and sees whether it fits, the result helps them understand their own judgment. A win at the end of a battle may reveal much less about whether one earlier target-selection decision was good. Ask how quickly a player sees the consequence, how clearly they can connect it to their choice, and how often they can try again within a session. [Core Loop, 10:10–16:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtqS8bLGJ3c&t=610s)

Loops can also connect across rounds. The hosts discuss a persistent resource that is accumulated now and spent later as a way to give earlier actions consequences in subsequent rounds. Persistence should create an understandable connection, rather than bookkeeping that merely survives a reset. [Core Loop, 8:50–10:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtqS8bLGJ3c&t=530s)

**Practical application:** describe the loop in verbs, build the smallest prototype that contains it, and test whether players want another cycle. Identify what your game improves or emphasizes. Novelty is useful when it creates the intended decisions; it is not an objective by itself.

## Turn an idea into a cheap playable prototype

Capture why the idea excites you. Mike describes writing a title, date, question and rough bullet points; Glenn keeps short ideas and revisits them after their initial excitement has cooled. Mike often needs to put an idea physically on a table before he can step away from it. These are different workable processes, rather than competing universal prescriptions. [Notebook to Tabletop, 1:50–8:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgDdV11MlGE&t=110s)

The first prototype should expose connections that were only imagined. Writing a complete procedure forces a reference such as “see the movement rules” to connect to an actual movement procedure. Playing it can reveal that a component is required in two places at once, that a phase lacks a necessary input, or that every apparently interesting turn has one obvious choice. [Notebook to Tabletop, 8:40–15:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgDdV11MlGE&t=520s)

Familiar mechanics can temporarily support an experiment. If the interesting question concerns movement, a known shooting procedure may be enough to let the prototype function. That is a way to reach the experiment sooner, not evidence that the borrowed procedure is the right final answer. Components and supporting rules still need to interact coherently. [Notebook to Tabletop, 11:00–13:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgDdV11MlGE&t=660s)

Use the tools that make revision easy. The discussion includes handwritten cards, card sleeves, erasable pens, digital layouts, printed rules and reference cards. Comfortable tools and rapid changes matter more than a particular software package. A miniature game may initially live largely in its rules document, while a component-driven game may reveal much of its design through the actual cards and pieces. [Notebook to Tabletop, 12:00–20:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgDdV11MlGE&t=720s)

Show a functioning prototype to a trusted person early. A few solo runs can establish that it hangs together; another person can then challenge the choices and the supposed source of fun. There is a useful qualification: know enough about the intended experience to judge whether the feedback addresses your game. An extremely early draft may not yet give you that judgment. [Notebook to Tabletop, 20:30–25:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgDdV11MlGE&t=1230s)

**Protect the purpose while remaining willing to replace its implementation.** Glenn stresses finding and testing the mechanical heart that the rest of the game supports. Mike also describes cases in which a supposedly central idea became expendable once more enjoyable ideas emerged. Their difference is worth retaining: commitment keeps a design coherent, but attachment can prevent a better version from emerging. [Notebook to Tabletop, 24:40–27:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgDdV11MlGE&t=1480s)

Keep prototypes inexpensive and disposable. Paying for attractive production too early can make a designer reluctant to remove a weak component. Save earlier versions so that a major experiment feels reversible. Version labels also let remote testers establish which rules they used. Small fixes and a replaced core system need not be treated as the same kind of change. [Notebook to Tabletop, 27:10–35:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgDdV11MlGE&t=1630s)

A failed project need not be wasted work. A useful mechanic, a discovered failure mode or a better understanding of a design problem can survive after the larger game is shelved. Salvaging those lessons does not require finishing an experience that is not working. [Notebook to Tabletop, 4:40–6:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgDdV11MlGE&t=280s)

## Choose activation by its consequences

Activation determines who acts, what they can act with, how much they can do and when the opportunity passes. A short rules section can therefore reshape the entire game. Initiative, activation quantity, action restrictions and turn-ending conditions interact even when a rulebook gives them separate headings. [Activation Systems, 0:40–5:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=40s)

| Family | Main opportunity | Main costs to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole-force turns | A player can form and execute a coherent operation without continually rebuilding the plan | First-turn damage and long waits for the opponent |
| Alternating units | Players receive frequent opportunities to respond to a changing situation | Repeated handovers and reassessment; concentrated alpha strikes can still exist |
| Alternating groups or phases | A compromise between very large and very small chunks of activity | How groups are formed and what remains predictable between phases |
| Random pulls | Uncertainty about who or what can act | Extreme streaks, restricted planning and the risk that one player receives very little opportunity |
| Simultaneous planning followed by resolution | Players make major decisions together on a stable state | Commitment rules, executability and the information available after plans are revealed |

[Activation Systems, 2:00–5:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=120s), [12:00–15:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=720s), [17:00–34:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=1020s)

### First turns and opportunities to reply

Whole-force turns become especially sensitive to initiative when starting positions and weapon reach allow substantial damage before the opponent has acted. Alternating units can reduce the quantity acting at once, but a powerful single activation can still remove an important enemy piece. Test deployment distance, reach, concentration of damage and the opportunity to reply together. [Activation Systems, 5:50–12:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=350s)

Delayed damage is one possible remedy. The hosts cite a system in which attacks assign damage during a round, while casualties resolve after both forces have acted. Other options include restricting first-turn activities, reducing immediate engagement or making initiative a tactical choice. Each remedy changes the experience, so choose one for the actual failure rather than assuming an activation family fixes it automatically. [Activation Systems, 8:40–11:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=520s)

### Downtime and handover time

Long opposing turns need a credible form of engagement: planning, reactions or a structure that returns decisions more often. The hosts also acknowledge a deliberately strategic design in which thinking through the opponent's turn is itself expected activity. Match that expectation to the audience. [Activation Systems, 11:50–15:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=710s)

Alternating activations create another cost. Every handover can require a player to ask what changed, reconstruct the plan and choose a unit again. Frequent turns can feel active while still adding substantial elapsed time. Observe that transition directly. Advance information, planned orders, a predictable upcoming activation or a simultaneous planning phase may preserve useful decisions while reducing repeated reconstruction. [Activation Systems, 17:00–21:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=1020s), [25:10–32:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=1510s)

### Random activation and planning

Randomizing the **player**, the **unit**, the **available action** and the **end of the round** are different choices. Separate pools can preserve player alternation while leaving the unit uncertain. A shared pool can make the next player uncertain. Replacement, exhaustion and reshuffling determine what streaks are possible. A rare sequence still occurs across enough games and should be considered during design. [Activation Systems, 21:10–25:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=1270s)

Finally, neither whole-army turns nor alternating units reproduce simultaneous reality without abstraction. The hosts disagree about which timing distortion feels stranger. Use that disagreement to resist a simple claim that one family is inherently more realistic. [Activation Systems, 14:40–17:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kW6isAJlFng&t=880s)

### Initiative and activation quantity are resources

A fixed order supports planning. Random, purchased and earned initiative create different incentives. Resource spending can turn a chance of priority into a decision, while activation tests and turnovers make sequencing safe and risky actions part of the challenge. Test the cost of failure: losing a bonus opportunity differs from losing the rest of a player's meaningful turn. [Initiative Systems](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=afUaBHlKfcI)

In a whole-force system, variable initiative can produce consecutive turns for the same army across a round boundary. In an alternating-unit system, a numerous force can spend cheap activations waiting for an elite opponent to commit. Attrition and summoning can change both problems. Compare whole-force consecutive turns, unit counts, group activations and delayed casualties separately rather than assuming all activation advantages are equivalent. [Initiative Systems](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=afUaBHlKfcI&t=360s), [Activation Advantage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m6b3SgJSPaM)

Pass opportunities for the smaller force can reduce the timing disadvantage without replacing the whole activation system. The hosts propose a stack of passes in the activation-count discussion. This preserves the chance to wait; it does not make unequal forces identical or replace testing the objective contest. [Activation Advantage, pass tokens](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m6b3SgJSPaM&t=755s)

Bag pulls need tests of extreme sequences as well as typical ones. Round-ending markers, uneven force sizes and long strings of one side's draws can deny opportunities. A reaction may give the waiting player a choice while still leaving them unable to move. Be explicit about what the physical bag adds over a deck or die, especially if tactile selection is not part of the procedure. [Bag Pulls](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hFZTOVvEqJ0)

## Choose uncertainty and components together

Luck and skill are not opposite ends of a single scale. A game can reward expertise while producing variable individual results. The useful questions are which skills matter, how consistently they affect results, and whether players can understand and manage the uncertainty. The hosts' comparisons are thought experiments and personal judgments, rather than measured rankings of games. [Luck versus Skill](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pgOB1QBVNXY&t=60s)

**Input randomness** presents a situation or resource before the player chooses what to do with it. **Output randomness** resolves uncertainty after a commitment. A randomly supplied hand can create an adaptation problem; a failed attack can deny a planned payoff. Neither category is automatically good or bad. A known risk can reward preparation, and the result of one action becomes the input to a later decision. Trace the complete sequence of choices and consequences. [Types of Randomness](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mfk8wsGVwYc&t=720s), [Luck versus Skill](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pgOB1QBVNXY&t=1020s)

Randomness can interrupt a solved plan, create suspense and support a shared story about an unlikely reversal. It can also produce a game in which someone never meaningfully acts. Audit failed activation chains, unusable opening resources and catastrophic late rolls separately. A result that makes previous decisions irrelevant has a different cost from a setback that leaves an interesting recovery. [Types of Randomness](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mfk8wsGVwYc&t=540s), [non-games](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mfk8wsGVwYc&t=1260s)

### Dice: desired outcomes before familiar procedures

Start with representative situations and the chance of success you want them to have. Then select a procedure that expresses those differences. The same basic success chance on a d6 and d20 does not imply the same frequency of critical faces, the same modifier sensitivity or the same space for differentiating units. A fixed bonus changes a smaller die's probabilities more strongly. Recheck the whole procedure after adding automatic results, rerolls or explosions. [Dice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dv4mal1zLN8&t=130s)

Be precise about what a pool does. **Summing results, counting successes and keeping the highest result are different probability systems.** A sum of independent dice concentrates around central totals; a success-counting pool has a distribution determined by its size and success threshold. Those observations do not justify saying that every pool behaves alike. Specify the operation, assess its actual odds and then test its physical handling. [Making Dice Pools Work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_jRw3-WTF8A&t=180s)

A player need not calculate every probability, but should have enough information to make the intended choice. Familiar dice can be easier to estimate than unusual mixtures. Counting visible successes may be easier than adding several modifiers, while a very large pool imposes acquisition, rolling, sorting and reading costs. Choose useful distinctions rather than the greatest available numerical precision. [Making Dice Pools Work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_jRw3-WTF8A&t=420s)

Exploding results and critical effects can create exciting outliers. Repeated rerolls also extend resolution, and special results can amplify the strongest units. A bounded extra-success effect may provide the desired spike with less handling. An opposed roll should justify the second player's roll through relevant information, interaction or experience; do not assume two rolls automatically make a richer decision. [Dice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dv4mal1zLN8&t=1740s), [opposed rolls](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dv4mal1zLN8&t=2520s)

### Cards: memory, choices and information

| Affordance | What it can provide | Questions to settle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rank, suit, colour and special cards | Several related outputs from one draw | Which signals matter, and can players read them quickly? |
| A finite deck | Memory, scarcity and changing possibilities | Is the discard visible? When does the deck reset? |
| A hand | Stored results, allocation, bluffing and selective intervention | How are cards drawn, held, spent and replenished? |
| Face-down commitment | Secret orders and simultaneous plans | When is a choice locked, revealed and executed? |
| Printed information | Unit references, events and setup instructions | Does this replace a lookup or add another component to manage? |
| Physical footprint | Measurement, zones, dashboards and markers | Are dimensions consistent and is there enough table space? |

[Normal Cards](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NuOvHuHJ-f4&t=180s), [More Uses for Cards](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8VFPFNmSAho&t=360s)

A trigger placed in a finite deck can make an event inevitable if the deck is exhausted while leaving its timing uncertain. That differs from an independent roll that can keep failing. Deck memory can support suspense and deliberate pacing, but also invites counting and discard inspection. Decide whether those are intended skills. [Normal Cards](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NuOvHuHJ-f4&t=690s), [trigger cards](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NuOvHuHJ-f4&t=1560s)

Cards carry richer information per item, while dice often resolve a large batch of attacks more conveniently. Standard cards lower the need for bespoke manufacturing, although players at a miniature club may carry dice more routinely than decks. Required custom randomizer decks also create access and replacement obligations: identical backs and a complete set matter in ways that optional reference cards do not. [More Uses for Cards](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8VFPFNmSAho&t=240s), [custom components](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8VFPFNmSAho&t=1020s)

### Use the physical object, not only its number

Dice can record wounds, turns and terrain states, conceal a small choice under a cup, become a reserve of rolled results, or supply coloured activation pulls. Their positions, orientation and shape can also carry information. These are design possibilities; several examples in the discussion are speculative. A physical trick needs readable handling rules and a reason to belong in the experience. [Other Uses for Dice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7_GvjZD3Dh0&t=300s)

State belongs somewhere. A marker beside a model makes information shared and immediate but clutters the scene. A side dashboard preserves the miniature view but moves information away from the decision. Track maxima should also fit the interface: a value above six is less convenient to display with one ordinary d6. [Other Uses for Dice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7_GvjZD3Dh0&t=90s)

## Make terrain, movement and deployment a connected system

An open miniature table is a physical rules interface. Players can place models at arbitrary positions and bring terrain whose windows, heights and shapes the designer never saw. Define which physical properties matter and how the players agree on their game meaning. The freedom is part of the medium's appeal, and it creates a corresponding need for robust spatial procedures. [The Medium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DTonRcCfXY&t=180s)

### Teach the table the rules expect

Give players enough guidance to build the intended battlefield: useful terrain examples, density, relevant sizes and a workable starter arrangement. An implied table known to the designer's group is not enough for someone learning elsewhere. Photographs and simple substitutes can communicate the intended problem more effectively than a vague request for a good amount of terrain. Terrain generation can also support the hobby, provided its result works with the game. [Deployment Phase](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=we5XkK7IS28&t=180s)

### Movement matters because positions differ

Unrestricted measured movement can still create rich play when exposure, objectives, threat ranges and control make destinations meaningfully different. Conversely, a complicated movement procedure does little if every destination is equivalent. One useful diagnostic from the hosts is to ask what exchanging two units' positions would change. [Movement Phase](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mX0cDWlOTuM&t=220s)

Different systems emphasize different skills. Free placement emphasizes finding a useful destination. Momentum, turning restrictions, templates and hidden movement commitments can emphasize prediction and execution. Restrictions can give a tank or vehicle physical character, but can also remove agency or generate a cascade of special cases. Retain the restrictions that create the intended experience. [Movement Phase](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mX0cDWlOTuM&t=1020s), [Movement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Lp3ZyXg1A8&t=540s)

Precision is not automatically depth. The hosts distinguish an interesting compromise between imperfect positions from a search for the single exact optimal spot. Test whether additional measurement supports judgment or merely demands more calculation. They also value declared intent when players agree that an intended legal position is achievable, so a small execution error need not obstruct the tactical choice. Keep terrain vocabulary and exceptions manageable. [Movement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Lp3ZyXg1A8&t=975s), [declared intent](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Lp3ZyXg1A8&t=1101s), [terrain vocabulary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mX0cDWlOTuM&t=1680s)

### Visibility and protection are separate design questions

True line of sight uses the model and scenery as physical evidence. It is intuitive in some situations but can make poses, decorative features and the observer's viewing angle consequential. Abstract visibility can use bases, footprints or agreed volumes instead. That reduces some modeling ambiguities while requiring explicit conventions, especially for height and unusual terrain. [Line of Sight](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yTltjVX1SJg&t=80s)

Being visible need not mean being unprotected, and protection need not be expressed as a conventional cover modifier. Choose how the game represents exposure and survivability rather than adding a familiar cover rule by default. Percentage-based cover can look precise while still requiring subjective judgments. Every exception should earn the attention it consumes. [Line of Sight](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yTltjVX1SJg&t=650s)

### Deployment establishes the opening question

Opposite edges create an approach to contact; close entry points can start at the contest itself. Alternating placement gives opportunities to counter-deploy, while larger placement blocks can be quicker. Neither structure is neutral when units have hard counters, limited movement or enough reach to dominate immediately. Test deployment with the actual scenario, not as a detached fairness procedure. [Deployment Phase](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=we5XkK7IS28&t=930s), [Better Deployment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aUfb5j3u1ps&t=900s)

A point plus a radius can act as a portable deployment zone. Measuring relative to a central location rather than only table edges can support different table shapes and several players. A distinctive opening may justify departing from convention, but first-game disasters and unexpected loss of approach turns still need testing. The hosts' racing-grid example deliberately creates an opening collision risk; it is a specific experience choice. [Better Deployment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aUfb5j3u1ps&t=120s)

### Hidden movement needs an information procedure

Concealment on continuous table space needs more than a statement that a unit is hidden. Explain how its location is recorded, how legal movement is verified, what causes a reveal, and who learns that a search succeeded. Blips, a finite set of candidate locations, decoys or an uncertainty area can simplify that task. They each sacrifice or complicate something else. [Hidden Movement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zzgdqZGY8UQ&t=180s)

Useful decoys create a real investment decision rather than an obviously wasted move. Imperfect searches can make a negative result uncertain instead of conclusive. Distinguish ordinary concealment from a procedure that also hides whether the opponent has detected the unit. Severe information asymmetry needs particular care: the uninformed player should still have meaningful choices and understand that uncertainty is part of the promised game. [Hidden Movement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zzgdqZGY8UQ&t=600s)

## Make combat resolve a worthwhile question

Combat can be the main attraction, or the payoff to earlier positioning, deployment and resource decisions. A simple attack procedure can work when the interesting question was how to arrange the fight. Complexity inside the attack roll should support the game's focus, rather than compensate for a battlefield that offers no consequential choices. [Interesting Combat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3NBBDuyhrVU&t=120s)

Rock-paper-scissors relationships, objective specialists and overlapping threat zones can make target selection matter. The most dangerous unit may be the one about to score, rather than the one with the biggest weapon. Flanks, engagement and disengagement can connect movement to combat, but each positional benefit brings interpretation or handling costs. Make roles and threats legible enough for an informed choice. [Interesting Combat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3NBBDuyhrVU&t=360s)

Test range in relation to table size, movement and deployment. Test melee's payoff against the risk and time required to reach it. A charging unit need not receive a separate charge bonus if the system already makes the desired attack worthwhile; bonuses should solve an actual incentive problem. [Combat Phase](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j5w0IB2qeGk&t=600s)

Every resolution stage needs a purpose. Hit, wound and save rolls can provide differentiation, suspense or defender participation, but also add handling. A limited resource, reaction or result intervention may give the defender a more consequential choice than another automatic roll. Decide what kind of participation is needed before selecting the procedure. [Combat Phase](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j5w0IB2qeGk&t=1000s), [Interesting Combat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3NBBDuyhrVU&t=1380s)

Signature actions need competitive payoffs within the intended context. A cinematic shove, trip or environmental interaction disappears from normal play if a routine attack is consistently easier and better. The hosts' shipboard example makes positioning and a later change in the environment turn a trip into a valuable action. Reward the behavior the game's fiction promises. [Interesting Combat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3NBBDuyhrVU&t=1560s)

### Give shooting a deliberate role

The shooting discussion challenges a familiar failure: a static gunline gains efficient damage while the opposing force crosses open ground and loses options. It is a critique of that experience, rather than a literal finding that every published shooting game fails. Test ranged-heavy forces, early reach, terrain, objectives and the recipient's decisions together. Suppression, displacement, restricted arcs, minimum ranges and lower lethality offer different ways to make fire shape movement instead of only deleting models. Each needs support from the rest of the game. [Shooting](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oXg_k-hnbiI)

### Damage is both a model of harm and an interface

Choose damage granularity according to the number of independently managed elements and the decisions each state creates. Several miniatures can be one functional unit; a handful of vehicles may support component damage. Casualty removal, posture, counters, tracks and dashboards distribute recordkeeping differently. More detailed damage is a cost even when it sounds physically plausible. [Damage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WFrPm84xZPU&t=120s)

Degradation can create a downward spiral in which surviving units retain activations but lose interesting choices. An alternative discussed by the hosts preserves capability while increasing the risk of final destruction. A wounded model that crawls toward rescue or an objective has a purpose; a model taking repeated ineffective turns may simply prolong the game. [Damage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WFrPm84xZPU&t=780s)

Healing and repair are strongest when their timing competes with another worthwhile action. Routine full resets can undo progress and extend play. Critical tables can create memorable scenes, but add lookups and states. The hosts differ over how much that spectacle justifies the interruption: preserve the preference distinction rather than declaring all tables a mistake. [Damage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WFrPm84xZPU&t=1260s)

Removal itself is optional. Displacement, temporary incapacitation, rescue, control or objective effects can change the situation without permanently reducing someone's available pieces. With a small crew, losing one miniature can remove a substantial share of a player's decisions. A game that retains pieces still needs pressure and a clear endpoint; simply making everything survive does not supply either. [Non-removal](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RByi1Jyvy10&t=240s)

## Design objectives, pacing and endings together

Victory conditions tell players what the game actually rewards. Write and test them early: a satisfying combat engine cannot rescue a trivial or tedious route to victory. Killing already provides a benefit by denying enemy actions and access to objectives. Additional kill points can reward the same success twice and displace the intended objective contest. [Writing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xMzPFNzemr4&t=1020s)

A scenario needs a setup, any in-game events or special procedures, an end condition and a method for determining the result. Explain force restrictions and unusual terrain before players arrive. A specific cast or hobby project can be an excellent planned scenario while being unusable for a spontaneous meeting between existing rosters. [Writing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xMzPFNzemr4&t=480s)

Scenario variety should ask different questions. It can spotlight movement, retrieval, protection, escape or a different use of the system; a required quota encourages redundant variants. Universal special rules can also affect forces unequally because speed, range and composition differ. Test those interactions, and make the challenge understandable at the table. [Writing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xMzPFNzemr4&t=1560s)

Procedural combinations of setup and objectives can reward adaptation instead of a single prepared list. They also place learning and choice at the start of the session. Supply a stable entry scenario before asking a newcomer to navigate the whole menu. Familiar default scenarios are socially useful: they reduce negotiation and preparation, which helps explain why alternatives can remain unused. [Designing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=efVHaNo50MA&t=900s)

There is no universally correct round count. The relevant measures are meaningful activity, opportunities to use appealing units, mental intensity and actual elapsed time. Rounds can provide a useful progress indicator, but only if their pacing supports it. Explain how force size and optional formats affect duration. [Game Length](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UUgKFJ1ru7k&t=40s)

End near the resolution of the central contest. Extra turns after the important question has been answered can become cleanup, incidental point collection or a slow anticlimax. A short game can still disappoint if a signature unit never gets to act, so test both the endpoint and the opportunities before it. [Game Length](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UUgKFJ1ru7k&t=480s), [Writing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xMzPFNzemr4&t=1800s)

A known final turn changes incentives: players can abandon longer-term safety or make contrived last-turn moves. An uncertain endpoint can reduce that behavior, while a fixed endpoint supports planning and scheduling. An objective-triggered ending offers another relationship between the central question and duration. Test what each ending invites players to do. [Writing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xMzPFNzemr4)

Draws can have meaning in a league, campaign or asymmetric mission. If a tiebreak measures something valuable, consider making that criterion visible during play so players can pursue it. A dramatic final minigame needs to fit the game's tone and skills; it is not a universal repair for an unsatisfying result. Narrative closure can also exist beyond the score. [Making Draws Fun](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6oyOMTQnVtM&t=360s)

### Solo and cooperative play need their own source of pressure

Identify what the second human contributes: opposition, uncertainty, disruption, prediction or social inference. A published solo mode must replace that function or offer a different satisfying challenge. Some games may be better without one. An automaton that exercises rules for the designer is not automatically an enjoyable opponent for a player. [Designing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=efVHaNo50MA&t=180s), [More Solo Mode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6hUpMP8SklU&t=0s)

Automate the opponent's effect with the smallest useful procedure. A clock, task, hazards and limited resources may provide enough pressure without a human-like AI. Long instruction sequences can move attention from the player's decisions to administration. A consistent simple opponent is useful as a development baseline, but cannot establish balance against intelligent adaptive play. [More Solo Mode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6hUpMP8SklU&t=360s)

Graded outcomes and persistent rewards can sustain effort after basic success becomes likely. Make partial success meaningful rather than turning every modest win into a disguised failure. Document any automated advantages, and keep separate the procedures being tested from the opponent behavior used to provoke them. [Designing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=efVHaNo50MA&t=600s), [More Solo Mode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6hUpMP8SklU&t=720s)

An automated opponent needs priorities and complete tie-breaking. Always pursuing the nearest unit invites baiting; pure randomness can produce implausible indecision. A short ordered set of criteria can make the behavior more purposeful, with random selection as a final fallback. Simplify automated unit options so the human spends their time on their own decisions. Reactions and threat zones can create pressure without asking the AI to plan like a person. [AI Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dv_ZykdY6Mo)

Make enemy setup quick and offer difficulty choices that fit the audience. A challenging mastery exercise and a relaxed narrative showcase serve different needs. A headline monster should also behave recognizably: efficient tactics are not the only test of whether it delivers the promised antagonist. [From PvP to PvE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IhC7eQbsj24)

## Use morale to create the intended behavior

Morale is a design choice. It may be important to the expectations surrounding frightened human troops, while a machine-focused or highly cinematic game may gain little from another way to lose actions. Ask what the subsystem contributes to this subject, scale and tone. [Do Games Need Psychology?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T5kJZYtUlds&t=0s)

Judge an effect by its trigger, frequency, duration and effect on agency. A brief disruption, a persistent refusal to act and a passive fear aura can all be called psychology while doing very different work. An aura may shape many movements without ever making a unit flee. A rare but catastrophic test may demand mitigation; immunity should remain a choice rather than becoming a mandatory roster tax. [Do Games Need Psychology?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T5kJZYtUlds&t=180s)

The hosts offer an analytical vocabulary: fear associated with a threatening presence, panic following an event, refusal tied to a unit trait, collapse or break tests, and distinctive behaviors such as frenzy or betrayal. This is their organizing scheme, not a universal taxonomy or a requirement to include every category. Positive inspiration and target preferences may also express character. Describe the actual trigger and behavior before choosing a familiar label. [Writing Psychology Rules](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LP7Dj0_zYN8)

Morale cascades can end a battle dramatically and reduce cleanup. They can also turn an early advantage into an irreversible collapse. A frightened car, spaceship and person need not react through the same movement procedure. Preserve the intended fiction and examine how many useful choices remain after the effect. [Do Games Need Psychology?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T5kJZYtUlds&t=360s)

## Build forces that support the table game

Roster construction is a form of play between sessions. It can support planning, collection, modeling, identity and repeated matchup discovery. It can also become homework or decide a match before the first move. Decide how much of the player's success should come from preparation and how much from decisions at the table. [Factions and List Building](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-0W6yuqPcAw&t=240s), [The Cursed Nature of List Building](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L2QHtCoEmYM)

Begin core-system tests with provisional, comparable forces. Testing one force against itself reduces the number of explanations for a failure. An enjoyable shared engine is a stronger foundation for asymmetry than a dull game awaiting exciting faction rules. This is a development sequence, not a claim that every finished game needs symmetrical factions. [Factions and List Building](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-0W6yuqPcAw&t=60s)

Situational strengths and weaknesses can create meaningful choices without making every option universally equivalent. Players need discoverable responses to strong strategies and enough information to understand roster risks. Hidden traps are particularly costly when a rules choice has already become an assembled and painted miniature. Physical commitment makes a bad choice less reversible than replacing an abstract card in a draft. [The Cursed Nature of List Building](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L2QHtCoEmYM)

| Construction approach | Useful purpose | Cost or risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed or recommended forces | Fast entry, controlled learning and fewer balance variables | Less pregame expression |
| Broad packages or bounded menus | Personal choice with less fine configuration | Less precise tailoring |
| Detailed point-based construction | Optimization, identity and extensive between-session play | Homework, interactions and potential traps |
| Scenario-aware selection | Adaptation to a known problem | Longer setup and an advantage from owning more options |
| Drafting or constrained random selection | A shared construction activity and unfamiliar combinations | Uneven results or additional setup procedures |

These are choices to compare, rather than a progression toward a superior final format. [Factions and List Building](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-0W6yuqPcAw&t=2220s), [The Cursed Nature of List Building](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L2QHtCoEmYM)

### Balance includes the environment and the community

A meta is the interaction among choices and the responses players discover. A strong approach with a plausible counter creates a different problem from an unanswered dominant list. Stability is not necessarily stagnation if repeat matchups remain enjoyable. More content is not automatically required to keep a game worth playing. [Designing the Meta](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D7u-kmwuY04)

Local groups can disagree sharply about the same option. Record the scenarios, opponents and community behind a balance claim. Broad data and sustained analysis are demanding for a small publisher. Mutable digital points can support corrections, but also require a clear authority, maintenance capacity and player trust. Do not treat the ability to update as evidence that an update is justified. [Designing the Meta](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D7u-kmwuY04&t=240s)

Published optimized lists can compress the discovery game a designer imagined. The tabletop experience should still work when players share solutions or converge on a small set of builds. Distinct factions can give players a useful matchup puzzle after defeat, but that interpretation must not conceal a genuinely unwinnable pairing. [Factions and List Building](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-0W6yuqPcAw&t=1860s)

## Make failure consequential without emptying the game

Audit exchanges in which nothing changes. A missed action, repeated save or cancelled effect may consume handling and anticipation without moving position, resources, risk or future options. Negation can have a valid control role, but repeated dead moments deserve scrutiny. Alternatives include a cost, redirection, displacement, delay or an uncontrolled consequence. [Something from Nothing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CCSUM-fHN2Y)

Failure can change tempo and position while leaving the piece and its interesting choices available. Knockdown, recovery and assistance can create temporary setbacks instead of permanent removal. They also require an endpoint and enough lasting change to prevent an endless cycle of falling and standing. Compare the cost in decisions, bookkeeping and elapsed time. [Impactful Failure without Win-more](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ya77wPgJ-1A)

### Separate three comeback goals

1. **Continued participation:** the trailing player still has useful actions.
2. **A credible reversal:** the player still has a route to victory.
3. **A satisfying finale:** even a likely loss has a worthwhile last decision or spectacle.

One mechanism may provide only one of these. A close-looking score is not proof of meaningful agency, and a spectacular last stand need not equalize the players' chances. Diagnose the actual problem before adding a bonus. [Your Game Needs a Catch-up Mechanic](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jxTuWF1a8jE), [Designing Catch-up Mechanics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CuEZVnOLuls)

Objectives, utility units, reserves or positional costs can keep a weakened force relevant without restoring its combat strength. Recovery resources need to exist when the setback occurs; a starting pool already spent cannot help later. A flexible resource may provide a recovery use for the trailing player and a different useful choice for the leader. These are options to test, not a requirement for a new currency. [Designing Catch-up Mechanics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CuEZVnOLuls)

**Do not make pursuing victory irrational.** The hosts describe an early racing design in which leading was punished so strongly that experienced players avoided the front. Refinement turned that problem into a tense finish while preserving a reason to race. Watch what skilled players actually do, rather than assuming the incentive printed in the rules produces the intended behavior. [Features and Flaws](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jrf_g89eprg), [Catch-up Mechanics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CuEZVnOLuls)

Natural balancing effects depend on scale. A slow formation that pursues a beaten enemy may become isolated from the main contest. A highly mobile skirmisher on a small table may immediately threaten another objective. Do not import assumptions about recovery from a different movement and battle structure. [Your Game Needs a Catch-up Mechanic](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jxTuWF1a8jE)

## Give campaigns a purpose and a sustainable arc

Decide whether campaign play is the game's central promise, an optional extension or an unnecessary addition. Some players value imagining a continuing force even when they rarely finish campaigns. That aspiration may matter, but it is not evidence that a long campaign chapter delivers more value. A campaign that drives the premise should influence the battle engine early. [Why Campaigns?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rihuqsIcn30)

Separate **persistent force progression** from **connected narrative**. A force can change without a scripted story, and a series of linked scenarios can tell a story without increasing anyone's stats. Treat them as separate design decisions before combining them. [Narrative Campaigns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SkPcJk244sA&t=240s)

### Progression, setbacks and participation

Equipment, scars, injuries and advancement can memorialize events players already care about. A cosmetic record may preserve a story without permanently weakening a beloved figure; a harsher consequence may suit a deliberately severe game. Test whether the player still wants the next session after repeated setbacks. [Campaign Progression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8rLqTB5EEg4)

Follow the reward curve across several wins and losses. Giving the winner more future strength while removing the loser's assets compounds an initial gap. Possible brakes include capped gains, baseline support, recovery and unlocking options rather than simply adding power. Broader options still confer flexibility, so an unlock is not automatically balance-neutral. [Campaign Progression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8rLqTB5EEg4)

A brake that only delays the same inevitable runaway has not solved that problem. The hosts also challenge random deprivation being defended as story: unequal rewards can create anecdotes while denying access to the models, equipment or content a player wanted. Distinguish an interesting consequence from a frustrating restriction. [Campaign Progression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8rLqTB5EEg4&t=703s), [random rewards](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8rLqTB5EEg4&t=894s)

Growth can record what a character actually did. A short achievement checklist can connect advancement to action, while different useful reward paths can give both successful and struggling players something to anticipate. Campaign victory and roster growth can also follow separate tracks, so developing a force does not automatically move it closer to winning the wider competition. These choices need their own rules and testing. [Campaign Progression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8rLqTB5EEg4&t=1160s), [separate goals](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8rLqTB5EEg4&t=1837s)

Real groups miss sessions. Decide how absent or returning players can rejoin without making either attendance or ordinary life feel punished. Reward limits and baseline participation rules need to preserve both a reason to win and a reason to continue after losing. [Campaign Progression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8rLqTB5EEg4)

### Narrative structures

| Structure | What it offers | Main work to account for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear sequence | A clear authored or historical arc | Each battle needs a purpose despite limited branching |
| Branching sequence | Outcomes change the next question | Additional writing, testing and viable less-likely paths |
| Campaign map | A visible context for who fights where and why | Organization, accumulated territory advantages and extra rules |
| Full strategic layer | A second scale of decisions | Designing and balancing the two games together |

A map can organize encounters without becoming a complete resource-and-conquest game. Even territory with no explicit combat bonus can accumulate a decisive scoring advantage. Test whether the extra layer increases enthusiasm for the next battle or turns ordinary games into an obligation for someone already behind. [Narrative Campaigns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SkPcJk244sA)

Campaign scenarios can record information, personal achievements, escape, loot or a changed future situation instead of only win/loss. Narrative is not a synonym for noncompetitive or deliberately unbalanced: asymmetry, preparation and persistent consequences are separate attributes. [Designing Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=efVHaNo50MA&t=1440s)

Authored scenarios reduce the invention required from players, but create obligations around cast, forces and roles. Explain who supplies or plays the antagonists and how new people enter. A tutorial sequence should help newcomers without forcing an experienced group to restart its entire story. Cooperative or solo presentation can suit a strongly prescribed narrative more readily than asking one opponent to serve as another player's disposable obstacle. [Narrative](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FqDy4m-xmTk)

Campaign validation is a separate undertaking. Isolated battles and hypothetical late-stage rosters can expose defects but miss interactions across a complete sequence. The hosts emphasize the time and coordination needed for end-to-end runs. Do not present a battle test as proof of a healthy campaign. [Why Campaigns?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rihuqsIcn30&t=540s)

## Design multiplayer and teams as distinct formats

Adding a third player changes incentives. Two opponents can spend resources weakening each other while an untouched rival benefits. Deployment, target access, scoring and the endpoint therefore need their own design work; a duel plus another force is not automatically a sound free-for-all. [Piggy in the Middle](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VyfFkJ7pko8)

Negotiation is a skill. Decide whether the game intends to reward it or whether persuasion is displacing the tactical decisions it promises. Visible standings and contestable objectives can give an in-game reason to target a leader. Several valuable contests can distribute pressure. Target restrictions can interrupt dogpiles but must still leave viable actions. [Political Problems](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G-TdPPaswXo), [Piggy in the Middle](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VyfFkJ7pko8)

When a player cannot win but can choose the winner, rules and expectations need to address the remaining purpose: improve placement, pursue a personal goal, exit, or retain a genuine recovery chance. No procedure removes every social choice while preserving full agency. Be clear about the intended experience. [Political Problems](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G-TdPPaswXo)

Simultaneous commitment and private information can reduce quarterbacking by preventing one player from confidently directing everyone's sequential decisions. They also reduce easy coordination and introduce surprise. Do not add concealment solely as an automatic cure; test the new experience as well as the old problem. [Political Problems](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G-TdPPaswXo)

For teams, shared objectives should create shared work. Requirements for contributions from several teammates, ally-directed resources, repair, assistance and passing objectives can make players attend to one another. Distinct roles need compatible incentives. A tactical optimizer and a player pursuing a cinematic moment can otherwise end up frustrated with each other's choices. [Team Breakdown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6gJmR67sJYA)

## Spend complexity where the experience needs it

Distinguish learning new procedures from thinking during play. A familiar system may create demanding live calculations; a novel subsystem may become easy once learned. Audit arithmetic, lookups, remembered states, independent entities and repeated reassessment separately. The number of decision-bearing groups often matters more than the number of physical models. [Cognitive Load](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GxV9cguZ9Y0)

Use familiar supporting procedures to leave attention for the distinctive idea. A novel mechanism earns its cost when it creates a new choice, relationship or outcome. A different sequence that recreates the same result may add learning without benefit. The hosts' suggestion to foreground only a few major innovations is a heuristic, not a validated universal numerical limit. [Pressure and Complexity](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qTpiFtBmbhk)

Make public state readable. Persistent values should not be hidden merely to test memory. Tokens, references and dashboards can reduce recall and computation, but excess markers create their own visual burden. Ask whether someone can understand the relevant battlefield by looking at it. [Cognitive Load](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GxV9cguZ9Y0)

**Bloat differs from power creep.** One accumulates too many options or procedures; the other makes later content stronger. A new option should have a distinct role. Its testing cost includes interactions with existing content, and a universal addition has a wider reach than an isolated module. An optional label alone cannot control how communities incorporate it into normal play. [Avoiding Bloat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jbVEKA0tReM)

Scope is a powerful complexity tool. A bounded environment can avoid rules for many things that never need to occur. Components can carry reminders or rules, but moving text onto cards does not make the total system smaller. More pages and options are not automatic generosity. Curate what supports the promised experience. [How Many Rules?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BCh6jTozeEI)

### Borrow mechanics with a clear connection to the battlefield

**Dice allocation:** roll resources, then choose their assignments. Keep basic participation possible with poor results; make different pools suggest different viable plans. Rerolls, conversion or sacrificing one result to reshape others can mitigate a bad pool, at the cost of another decision layer. Track available and committed dice visibly. Connecting future pools to current positions can integrate the systems, but may also amplify a lead. [Dice Spend](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H3piagVcDyU)

The hosts argue that this allocation puzzle is substantial enough to deserve a central place if it is used. Mike describes removing it from A Billion Suns when other decisions proved more important. They also favor open allocation in this discussion: discussing visible results can be communal, while concealed dice sacrifice that interaction and are easier to alter accidentally. These are reasons to test the whole experience before adding a hidden reveal. [Dice Spend, central role](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H3piagVcDyU&t=1150s), [public allocation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H3piagVcDyU&t=1381s)

**Push your luck:** the player chooses whether to stop or risk accumulated value for more. A risky resolution with no continue/stop choice is a different mechanism. Preserve a useful safe option, limit runaway sequences and test incentives from ahead and behind. A trailing player forced to gamble more can fall further behind. Visible failure consequences can make the risk a shared spectacle. [Push Your Luck](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E0DIj1aOY4I)

**Deckbuilding:** distinguish constructing a deck before play from changing it during play. Card additions, removals and replacements can express specialization, attrition, faction identity or campaign history. State whether the deck belongs to a model, unit or force, and make its changes coherent at that scale. Large action chains must still leave meaningful battlefield decisions. [Deckbuilding](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DEXPYDM-brM)

**Dexterity:** a physical action can embody a fiction rather than merely describe it. Stacking, sliding and dropping components can create persistent tension and visible feedback. Dice size, grip, table friction, player ability and the handling of painted models then become part of the design environment. Several examples are experiments, rather than validated ready-to-use systems. [Dexterity](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R5fY2BwgubE)

## Use theme and fiction to support actual play

Name the moments the game should produce, translate them into table actions, make those actions possible and give players a reason to choose them. A name or paragraph of fiction cannot produce a feeling the procedures fail to support. The repeated decisions should carry the central fantasy. [Creating Theme](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OVJ8SGDKoBI)

Genre expectations can become test questions: did the death race produce dangerous driving and enjoyable chaos? Did a supposedly heroic game let a minor mishap erase its central character before anything interesting happened? Different fiction contracts justify different outcomes; heroic emulation and harsh uncertainty make different promises. [Creating Theme](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OVJ8SGDKoBI), [Plot Armour](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8Vti7Ck0uT8)

Plot support is easier to author against a solo system than against another human who deserves worthwhile goals. Finite survival resources, differentiated vulnerabilities and incentives can make desired moments more likely without guaranteeing a fixed story. If the headline result is deliberately predetermined, a meaningful secondary question can determine the consequences for a later scenario. State whether players are competing or collaborating on a reenactment. [Plot Armour](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8Vti7Ck0uT8)

A concise genre cue may be enough for a self-contained game. A long-running campaign or character-led product may need richer relationships. Leave room where players are meant to invent forces and stories. Lore can teach a tactical role, suggest models and terrain, and make a rule easier to remember; it can also become a separate reading burden with little connection to play. [Worldbuilding](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KKijQHXVqEE)

Build a usable world rather than an exhaustive encyclopedia. A concrete conceit and rough map can expose relationships and conflicts; short scenes or conversation can test whether a location feels inhabited. Players need enough practical coherence to build armies and decide why they fight. Private exploratory writing can help the designer without all of it belonging in the published book. [Creating a World](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CPJCjUWxA2A)

Visible and off-screen events create different expectations. A model's apparent size and position constrain what a physical move seems to mean; unseen activity inside a ship can be abstracted more freely. Choose the level of representation that supports the desired cinematic view. [Creating Theme](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OVJ8SGDKoBI)

### Let the medium justify its effort

Miniatures offer spatial judgment, a changing three-dimensional scene, customization and a shared creative performance. Those pleasures can matter even beyond competition. They also bring collection, preparation, measurement and handling. Compare a paper or card prototype and ask which valuable experience disappears without the figures and open table. [Why Miniatures? Skills](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fix8CfDYsd4), [Why Miniatures? Looks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_xP5uIsYg8Q)

Repeated mastery and between-session hobby work suit some audiences. A focused first experience with limited expected replay suits others. Investment in models raises the stakes of onboarding: a newcomer may already have spent considerable effort before the first game. State the expected commitment and make that first session demonstrate the appeal. [Why Miniatures? Growth](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4dhCZBpspGs), [Different Experience Levels](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KDfghDhCAWY)

## Recognize tradeoffs that cannot be patched away

The hosts use **cursed problem** for desirable goals that undermine each other. Late reversals can conflict with the weight of earlier decisions; severe failure can conflict with continued agency; deep roster optimization can conflict with accessible tactical play. Repeated patches can simply move the pain. Name the competing goals and choose a position appropriate to the audience. [This Game Is Cursed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DR_YX-BuHic)

A decisive early victory can be valid when the game clearly promises that risk. Another game may deliberately preserve a long dramatic contest. Neither needs to satisfy the other's audience, but a designer should still investigate recurring friction rather than dismissing it as players using the game incorrectly. [This Game Is Cursed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DR_YX-BuHic), [Features and Flaws](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jrf_g89eprg)

### Rules can accidentally invite behavior

Putting a price on unwanted conduct can imply permission to do it whenever the advantage exceeds the penalty. The dice-tower examples explore how an accident rule can make deliberate interference appear to be a legitimate move. Prefer a simple restoration or state procedure that does not reward causing the accident. [Proscription and Permission](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vQNDHc71xs&t=429s)

Common physical mishaps deserve clear defaults: displaced models, unstable positions and cocked dice can recur in ordinary play. That does not require enumerating every implausible form of misconduct. Explain the recurring interactions while keeping actions outside the intended activity outside its formal economy. [Proscription and Permission](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vQNDHc71xs&t=1562s)

Sometimes an expected freedom bypasses the game's best activity. The affordances episode describes removing ordinary direct wounds from an early shipboard design so that positioning and environmental danger became central. Merely making stabbing inefficient had not stopped players choosing it. Removing an action, changing its priority and changing its reward are distinct interventions. Check each against the whole design: a nonlethal shooting replacement can preserve participation while undermining a separate goal of very fast play. [Affordances](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ao1l5G6-WOE&t=557s)

Model geometry is another deliberate choice. If banners, poses or decorative additions affect visibility and protection, hobby decisions acquire mechanical consequences. That can be intended, or it can undermine visual freedom. State which physical features count, how proxies work and what players should agree before play. Photographs and annotated examples help communicate the expectation. [Modeling for Advantage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o-BYSCBxu9Q)

## Write rules people can learn and consult

Keep authoritative procedure distinguishable from character commentary. An unreliable or opinionated in-world voice can enrich a sidebar while confusing a binding instruction. Tone can prepare players emotionally for harsh or comic events, but cannot replace clarity about what happens. Use direct verbs, consistent terms and genuine optional language only where the player has an option. [Finding Your Voice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qqDrQQ6Bg40)

Break a complex process into a trigger, a state or result, and the next step. A named condition can connect those steps when it recurs elsewhere; a one-off label can become needless vocabulary. The rulebook's job is to teach and state the game, while the pitch has a different promotional job. [Finding Your Voice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qqDrQQ6Bg40), [Additional Voices](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FGzcB6Bhrgg)

Distinguish an ambiguity that stops play from an odd result that still resolves. Exhaustive clauses for every unusual interaction can make the normal procedure harder to learn. Put frequent rules in the main text and use a findable FAQ or reference for rare clarifications. Numbering and cross-references are useful when they improve navigation, rather than merely making the book look formal. [Additional Voices](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FGzcB6Bhrgg)

Teach prerequisites before dependent systems, then preserve clear entry points for lookup. Drafting may reveal that the original order is wrong. The hosts' own book process involved substantial restructuring and repeated rewrites; polishing sentences cannot always repair a missing central explanation. [The Design Book](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9vwVLEKlweA)

When explaining a named game's exact procedure, consult its actual rules. The hosts themselves distinguish researched case studies from casual video recollection. This reference uses their examples to explain design arguments, rather than treating recollection as an authoritative substitute for another game's rulebook. [The Design Book](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9vwVLEKlweA&t=360s)

## Playtest the rules and the experience

Playtesting asks whether the connected game functions, whether its decisions are interesting, whether the rules communicate and whether it produces the intended feelings. A balanced outcome is only one part of that work. [How to Playtest, 1:50–4:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=110s)

### Use the appropriate kind of test

| Method | What it is particularly useful for | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mental analysis | Contradictions, impossible sequences and a mathematically clear defect | Whether people enjoy the connected experience |
| Solo physical play | Missing procedures, interactions, scenario coverage and basic coherence | Independent interpretation or normal social interaction |
| Observed group play | Actual choices, energy, confusion, expectations and interaction | Whether the rulebook works entirely without the designer |
| Blind play | Teaching, reference use, improvised interpretations and whether players continue independently | Every detail of the experience unless useful observations are captured |

Do not spend a full session proving a defect whose mechanism is already clear. Conversely, thinking a game works is not a substitute for watching other people play it. [Playtesting for Miniatures Games, 2:50–9:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SVZafm_WqkM&t=170s)

Solo-test enough to respect other testers' time. Play all sides as fairly as possible, say the reasoning aloud if that helps, and record missing procedures. Solo testing can be efficient, but preferences and familiarity can conceal problems. [Playtesting for Miniatures Games, 5:00–9:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SVZafm_WqkM&t=300s)

### Observe before rescuing the players

When someone asks how a rule works, find out how they think it works and watch them consult the book. An immediate explanation can hide a rule that was never written or wording that communicates something different. Taking the observer's role rather than playing also frees attention for notes and the social experience around the table. [How to Playtest, 5:50–8:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=350s)

Look for specific moments: excitement about a possibility, an unexpected prohibition, confusion, frustration, checking a phone or disengaging from the game. Ask what the player expected and why. A request for a cinematic maneuver does not automatically justify another subsystem; it reveals an expectation that the design should consciously address. [How to Playtest, 8:10–13:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=490s)

General praise is less diagnostic than a specific problem. Politeness can produce an enthusiastic answer without predicting whether someone would choose to play again. Questions about the most disappointing, confusing or weakest moment make a concrete discussion easier. [How to Playtest, 8:50–10:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=530s)

### Separate the symptom from the proposed solution

Take a tester's bad experience seriously without treating their proposed repair as mandatory. The designer needs to understand the problem and choose among remedies. The hosts also welcome good ideas from testers: the distinction concerns responsibility for the final design, rather than refusing outside suggestions. [How to Playtest, 19:40–23:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=1180s)

Keep a core of experienced testers and keep introducing fresh players. Experienced testers can isolate a changed subsystem and remember approaches already tried. Newcomers expose assumptions in teaching and presentation. Their first encounter happens only once, so a group that knows the game intimately cannot reproduce it indefinitely. [How to Playtest, 14:50–18:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=890s)

Repeated reminders are also evidence. A rule that players continually forget may need removal, rewriting, relocation or a better reminder. Glenn's suggestion to reconsider a rule after explaining it more than three times in a session is a personal diagnostic heuristic, not a universal threshold. [How to Playtest, 16:10–17:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=970s)

### Test without the designer

Blind testing asks whether people can play from the supplied rules when the creator is absent. Players may invent a ruling, stop or reject the experience in ways they would avoid in front of the designer. A recording can preserve the exact point of confusion and the assumption that sends play in another direction. Written reports remain valuable, especially when a specific event supports follow-up questions. [Playtesting for Miniatures Games, 9:10–16:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SVZafm_WqkM&t=550s)

A collapsed session need not continue to its nominal ending. If the failure is understood, stop, record it and discuss the design. Forcing another two hours of a broken experience can consume goodwill without answering a new question. [Playtesting for Miniatures Games, 26:30–28:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SVZafm_WqkM&t=1590s)

### Know the audience you are testing for

Be honest about the experience. A high player count can suggest a party game even when the actual game is complex and severe. A harsh elimination game may intentionally suit some players and repel others. The hosts differ in their usual preferences: Mike generally avoids elimination and disengagement, while Glenn describes a survival design where honest, avoidable elimination worked better than an unrewarding consolation role. [Playtesting for Miniatures Games, 22:00–26:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SVZafm_WqkM&t=1320s)

Treat testers as people whose time and trust matter. Test other designers' work to improve your own feedback habits. Eventually a project also needs a release or shelving decision: perpetual iteration is not the same as a plan to finish. The episodes' suggested test counts are practical opinions, not a certificate of readiness. [How to Playtest, 3:50–5:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=230s), [19:00–27:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WxmsMPyCmWk&t=1140s)

## Choose a production and publishing route that fits the project

A first product should fit the creator's production abilities and resources. Glenn describes selecting a comparatively manageable project from several appealing ideas. A larger project is not inherently wrong, but it needs the corresponding experience and team. Define success first: producing a personally important game, covering a print run, building a product line and earning a living are different objectives. [Pitch to Product, 1:25–6:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EaSDsNv21Xg&t=85s)

A publishing route also allocates work. Mike preferred a partner to handle production, distribution and marketing so that his limited time could remain focused on design. Glenn describes crowdfunding's ability, in his experience, to connect attention with an immediate commitment to support a product. These are reasons to compare routes, rather than guarantees of present-day commercial results. [Pitch to Product, 7:50–14:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EaSDsNv21Xg&t=470s)

For a publisher pitch, show both appeal and substance. Mike describes an energetic short abstract, an outline that demonstrates the proposed manuscript's shape, and a rules sample that shows he can deliver coherent work. His approximate 200-word abstract is an example, not a universal submission requirement. Research the recipient's product line and actual submission process; a refusal can concern fit, timing or the publisher's financial choices. [Pitch to Product, 14:00–22:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EaSDsNv21Xg&t=840s)

### Miniatures and partnerships

An independent designer can collaborate with an existing miniature producer instead of commissioning a whole range. Available models can inspire usable unit ideas, while a recommended range can make a miniature-agnostic game easier to start. State how essential that range is and what happens if availability changes. The hosts' discussion explores reciprocal support and STL arrangements, but does not provide a complete licensing or commissioning procedure. [Producing Miniatures](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bxWL7tqnSWY)

### Books and manufacturing

Local short runs, print-on-demand and larger offset runs support different quantities, finishes and financial commitments. Compare the physical product, intended audience and inventory exposure. A premium hardback is one possible identity; a deliberately inexpensive or home-printable format can also fit the game. Existing books can serve as concrete benchmarks when asking a printer to explain specifications and quotes. [Producing Books](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98Co_mdwcPw)

Small batches can teach the process and test demand before a larger investment. The hosts discuss inexpensive zines at a convention as a way to discover which ideas interest buyers. That is a practical experience-based suggestion, not a forecast of sales or a current price benchmark. [Producing Books](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98Co_mdwcPw)

Separate manuscript review, layout, print-file preparation and component manufacture. A fresh reader can find inconsistent terminology. Printers' trim, bleed and cover requirements need to be understood before final artwork and layout. Physically assembling a representative component can expose mirroring, material friction and fit problems that a flat image conceals. Specialist support and proofs have value, particularly when the creator is learning production. [Preparation for Publishing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LioJXWT6vd4)

The second-edition design discussion supplies another useful test: list everything needed to reach the first playable game, including miniatures, markers, terrain and learning. A well-regarded game can still be difficult to get onto the table. Revisions can remove clever but peripheral procedures and give the strongest connected systems more room. Optional modes should leave an obvious starting experience. [Second-edition Design Goals](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=694bwtpMcq8)

Commercial anecdotes in these episodes are historical. Use them to identify questions about production, support and distribution. Obtain current specifications and actual quotes when making a real product decision.

## A practical design worksheet

This worksheet is **editorial synthesis of the preceding discussions**. It is a way to turn the reference into a prototype and test plan; it is not a list of Settlements rulings or a verbatim method prescribed by the hosts. Follow the relevant sections above for the source arguments.

| Question | Record before expanding the design |
| --- | --- |
| Who is it for? | Expected experience, session context, hobby commitment and first-game expectations |
| What should it feel like? | A short promise and a few observable moments that would demonstrate it |
| Why miniatures? | The spatial, visual, physical or hobby contribution that justifies the medium |
| What repeats? | The core loop in verbs, the important choice and the feedback after it |
| What is distinctive? | The few mechanisms that carry the promise, plus familiar supporting procedures |
| How does the opening work? | Terrain expectations, deployment, reach, initiative and both players' first useful opportunity |
| What does uncertainty do? | When it enters, what players know, what can fail and how they can prepare or adapt |
| What makes positions valuable? | Objectives, exposure, threat, control, paths and the cost of changing position |
| What happens after a hit or failure? | Changed position, capability, risk, resources or opportunity; who makes the next decision |
| What ends the contest? | An explicit endpoint, scoring, draw handling and a check against empty cleanup |
| What can a trailing player do? | Continued useful actions, a real reversal if intended, or a worthwhile finale |
| What happens between battles? | Only the persistence needed for the intended campaign and hobby experience |
| What must players remember? | Public-state display, reference aids, unique conditions and repeated calculations |
| What must the rules teach? | Common procedure, consistent terms, examples, rare-case reference and independent play |
| What is the next test? | A specific uncertainty, appropriate test method and observable evidence |
| What can be cut? | Duplicate options, inherited assumptions, weak supporting rules and prototype scaffolding |

### A compact development sequence

1. Write the intended experience and repeated decision.
2. Build a cheap connected prototype containing that decision, an objective and an endpoint.
3. Solo-test for missing procedures and obvious contradictions.
4. Observe another person using the written rules; record choices, confusion and emotional response.
5. Revise the mechanism that causes the problem, then test whether the change addresses it.
6. Broaden terrain, forces, scenarios and player experience deliberately; include extreme sequences and late states.
7. Blind-test the teaching and reference material.
8. Test the complete campaign arc separately if persistence is included.
9. Match production and release work to the actual product and resources.

These stages can overlap. A meaningful question is more useful than repeating a test simply to increase a count. Keep a version record so the evidence belongs to the rules actually tested.

### Record a playtest in a few lines

- **Version and question:** which rules, and what uncertainty did this session examine?
- **Conditions:** players' experience, forces, scenario, terrain and any deliberate unusual setup.
- **Observed moment:** what happened, what the player expected and what they did next.
- **Interpretation:** a plausible mechanism, separated from the observation.
- **Proposed revision:** what changes and which other systems it might affect.
- **Next evidence:** what would show that the revision helped, and what tradeoff needs checking?

## Dos and donts to keep beside the prototype

The pairs below condense the sourced discussions; they are contextual prompts, not universal bans. The detailed sections explain when a contrary choice can be deliberate.

| Do | Reconsider |
| --- | --- |
| Define the experience and audience | Adding a subsystem because the genre usually has one |
| Make ordinary turns carry the theme | Relying on names and background to supply missing gameplay |
| Prototype the connected loop cheaply | Protecting a weak rule because art or production is already expensive |
| Give signature actions a viable purpose | Merely permitting a cinematic action that sensible players never choose |
| Test deployment, reach and first response together | Treating alternating activation as an automatic cure for alpha strikes |
| Evaluate the precise random procedure | Treating every dice pool or random draw as equivalent |
| Make uncertainty create decisions | Letting opening randomness routinely deny meaningful participation |
| Preserve useful choices after setbacks | Keeping pieces alive only to make ineffective activations |
| Distinguish participation from comeback | Assuming a close score means the trailing player has agency |
| Keep pursuing victory worthwhile | Punishing leaders until players avoid the game's objective |
| Give roster options clear roles and counters | Expanding choices without budgeting for their interactions |
| Follow campaign gains across repeated results | Calling one balanced battle a tested campaign |
| Observe players and ask about specific moments | Rescuing every misunderstanding before the rulebook can reveal it |
| Investigate the symptom | Treating every tester's proposed repair as the required solution |
| Use clear terms and visible public state | Rewarding memory of bookkeeping instead of the intended skill |
| Explain common physical edge cases | Pricing unwanted conduct as a purchasable tactic |
| Set production scope and inspect physical results | Treating a finished manuscript as a finished manufactured product |
| Keep a concrete question behind each test | Using an arbitrary test count as proof of readiness |
