# 🎲 Oathmark

> [!abstract] In one breath
> A fantasy mass-battle game whose persistent layer is a **kingdom** — and whose central insight is that **every other game answers *"what does this cost?"* while Oathmark answers *"what are you entitled to field at all?"*** Its kingdom never buys you a bigger army. It buys you a **wider menu**. That single decision is what makes a persistent base layer safe to bolt onto a points-buy game.

## Why it's here

It is **the closest published analogue to the Settlements meta-layer** that exists, and it answers our hardest open question — *what should a bigger settlement actually give you?* — with a rule we can adopt almost verbatim.

---
## The kingdom widens the menu

**Type:** Settlement · **Take:** ⭐ steal — the most important row in this vault

McCullough, verbatim **[FACT — Osprey designer blog]**:

> *"The kingdom might gain new territory, or it might have its territory occupied, but **when it comes to each game, the players are still playing to the same points value, and theoretically have an equal chance of winning.**"*

> **The kingdom never buys you a bigger army. It only buys you a wider menu.**

Growth is **lateral, not vertical.** A 20-territory kingdom and a 10-territory kingdom both field 2,500 points. The winner's advantage is **optionality** — better matchup answers, access to rarer units — never **mass**.

**Why it works.** It removes the one thing that makes persistent-progression games collapse: the compounding conversion of past wins into present force size. A player who has been losing for six games still shows up with a legal, full-strength army. **The campaign's stakes stay real without the games becoming unwinnable.**

**For Settlements.** This is already our locked principle — *"Credits buy what you own; the Credits you field are your Crew Rating"* — and Oathmark is the proof it scales to a full campaign layer. It settles what [[Settlement]] growth should pay out: **unlocks, not budget.** New or upgraded [[Structures]] should unlock craftable [[Weapons]] and equipment tiers, new [[Skill Paths]], new options — never a bigger [[List Building|Crew Rating]] cap. See also [[Territory]].

## Persist the place, not the army

**Type:** Settlement · **Take:** ⭐ steal

His stated reason for putting persistence on the territory rather than the army **[FACT]**:

> *"I quickly realised that I couldn't just have people's armies 'gain experience'… First off, **this would grow completely out of hand really quickly**… Then it hit me — players shouldn't focus on **armies, which are just temporary constructs for a given battle**, but upon **the kingdom which produced them**."*

**For Settlements — the honest tension.** We are deliberately doing **both**: persistent crews *and* a persistent settlement, because per-unit persistence is a stated differentiator ([[Ideas Inbox]]) and the emotional core of the campaign. McCullough's warning is that unbounded army persistence *"grows out of hand really quickly"* — which is exactly why our [[Progression]] layer needs a valve. **His answer is to remove army persistence entirely; ours has to be a cap or a tax** (see [[Trench Crusade#Cap the veterans, don't tax them]]). Recording the warning, not adopting the fix.

## Concentric rings and the rarity gate

**Type:** Faction · **Take:** ⚙️ adapt

The Kingdom Sheet is **five concentric circles** — explicitly *"not a map"* but *"a visual guide based on how far certain territories are from the seat of power."* Region 1 holds 1 territory (your capital), Region 2 holds 2, Region 3 holds 3, Region 4 holds 4 — **10 territories at creation** — and **Region 5 starts empty, reserved for conquest.**

There is **no cost and no currency in kingdom building.** There are slots plus a rarity gate that tightens toward the throne **[FACT]**:

| Region | Territories | From your capital's own race list | From any *other* race's list |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | your capital city (rarity 1) | — |
| 2 | 2 | rarity ≤ **2** | rarity ≤ **1** |
| 3 | 3 | rarity ≤ **3** | rarity ≤ **2** |
| 4 | 4 | **anything, no limitations** | **anything, no limitations** |
| 5 | 0 at start | conquest only | conquest only |

**Why it works — concentric permissiveness.** Your heartland is racially coherent *by construction*; your frontier is a free-for-all. **It produces thematic armies without a single "you may not take X" rule.** One table of rarity numbers replaces an entire chapter of faction restrictions.

**For Settlements.** [[Territory]] and [[Factions]]. A rarity number per territory type, tightening toward the settlement core, would give us thematic crews with no prohibition rules to write, argue about, or errata.

## Occupied, never destroyed

**Type:** Settlement · **Take:** ⚙️ adapt

Territory losses are **soft and reversible** — a territory is occupied, not razed. A bad run costs you access, not existence, and the campaign remains recoverable.

**For Settlements.** Relevant to raid outcomes in [[Settlement]] and [[Campaign]]. A raid that permanently destroys a structure is a campaign-ending event for the loser; a raid that **occupies** or **disables** it for N turns is the same drama with a floor under it.

---
## Four more mechanisms worth taking

**[FACT — from the Army Planner and Kingdom extract]**

1. **Two unlock grammars per territory.** Each of the 39 terrain types either unlocks a unit **uncapped** (*"you may purchase as many orc and goblin slaves as you can afford"*) or with a cap. One column, two behaviours.
2. **Caps that reward diversity.** The 4-unit limit doesn't carry across races — **so diversity is the only legal route to a wider army.**
3. **Heroes capped by buildings, not by a points percentage.** Your structures determine how many heroes you may field. *(Directly transferable: [[Structures]] gating Specialist/Leader counts is more thematic and less gameable than a % cap.)*
4. **Growth is a menu, so the menu has to be worth reading.** Lateral growth only feels like progress if the new options are genuinely different, not just numerically better — which puts the design load on [[Weapons]] and [[Skill Paths]] breadth.
