# 🎲 Oathmark

> [!abstract] In one breath
> A fantasy mass-battle game whose persistent layer is a **kingdom** — and whose central insight is that **every other game answers *"what does this cost?"* while Oathmark answers *"what are you entitled to field at all?"*** Its kingdom never buys you a bigger army. It buys you a **wider menu**. That single decision is what makes a persistent base layer safe to bolt onto a points-buy game. **The full core rulebook now confirms this in the rules text itself, not just a designer blog** — see [[Oathmark#Confirmed in the rulebook itself, not just the blog]] — and two supplements (Battlesworn, Bane of Kings) add the sharpest published answer yet to our own veteran price-vs-cap fork.

## Why it's here

It is **the closest published analogue to the Settlements meta-layer** that exists, and it answers our hardest open question — *what should a bigger settlement actually give you?* — with a rule we can adopt almost verbatim.

---
## The kingdom widens the menu

**Type:** Settlement · **Take:** ⭐ steal — the most important row in this vault

McCullough, verbatim **[FACT — Osprey designer blog]**:

> *"The kingdom might gain new territory, or it might have its territory occupied, but **when it comes to each game, the players are still playing to the same points value, and theoretically have an equal chance of winning.**"*

> **The kingdom never buys you a bigger army. It only buys you a wider menu.**

Growth is **lateral, not vertical.** A 20-territory kingdom and a 10-territory kingdom both field 2,500 points. The winner's advantage is **optionality** — better matchup answers, access to rarer units — never **mass**.

**Why it works.** It removes the one thing that makes persistent-progression games collapse: the compounding conversion of past wins into present force size. A player who has been losing for six games still shows up with a legal, full-strength army. **The campaign's stakes stay real without the games becoming unwinnable.**

**For Settlements.** This is already our locked principle — *"Credits buy what you own; the Credits you field are your Crew Rating"* — and Oathmark is the proof it scales to a full campaign layer. It settles what [[Settlement]] growth should pay out: **unlocks, not budget.** New or upgraded [[Structures]] should unlock craftable [[Weapons]] and equipment tiers, new [[Skill Paths]], new options — never a bigger [[List Building|Crew Rating]] cap. See also [[Territory]].

### Confirmed in the rulebook itself, not just the blog

The prior read of this note rested the headline finding on a designer blog quote. The full core rulebook states it as a rule, not a design comment **[FACT — Oathmark core rulebook, p.82]**:

> *"Now that you have a kingdom from which to muster units, you can begin to assemble an army. **Every game you play has a fixed number of points you can spend to purchase figures for your army.**"*
>
> *"The point system exists to facilitate a balanced game, in which both players have an equal chance to win based on the figures on the table."*

The Army Point Value Suggestion Table sets that fixed number by **occasion** (550 for a beginner game, 2,500 standard, 4,000 large, 6,500 epic) — never by kingdom size, territory count, or campaign standing. In *The Campaign* chapter, the same rule holds under a different name: at the start of each campaign turn, *"the attacking player names a point value and both players assemble an army with that point value"* **[FACT, p.155]**. Whoever names the number, both sides build to it. A 20-territory kingdom and a fresh 10-territory kingdom sit the same battle at the same points — the only thing that changed is which 20 (or 10) units are legal to bring.

**One documented crack in the rule, and it's instructive.** *Bane of Kings*' Land Purchase kingdom event is the sole place across all three books where growing the kingdom costs points: a kingdom may buy a new Region 5 territory outside of combat, but *"you receive 10% fewer points to build your army for the next battle"* **[FACT — Bane of Kings, p.34-35]**. The designer reserves the points-tax lever for exactly one case — territory gained by *purchase* rather than *combat* — which reads as a deliberate choice to keep earned growth free while pricing the one path that bypasses the game's actual currency (winning battles). See also [[Oathmark#Kingdom Events, and the one crack in "growth never costs budget"]].

**A second confirmation, from a different angle.** *Bane of Kings*' Special Formations chapter unlocks new *tactics for units you already own*, not new units, and says so explicitly **[FACT, p.7]**: *"The goal of these rules is to give players new ways to play and new strategies to try **without having to buy or paint new units**!"* Growth widening the menu isn't limited to new figures — it can widen the menu of things you're allowed to *do* with figures already in the list. See [[Oathmark#Reuse before purchase: formations before new figures]].

## Persist the place, not the army

**Type:** Settlement · **Take:** ⭐ steal

His stated reason for putting persistence on the territory rather than the army **[FACT]**:

> *"I quickly realised that I couldn't just have people's armies 'gain experience'… First off, **this would grow completely out of hand really quickly**… Then it hit me — players shouldn't focus on **armies, which are just temporary constructs for a given battle**, but upon **the kingdom which produced them**."*

**For Settlements — the honest tension.** We are deliberately doing **both**: persistent crews *and* a persistent settlement, because per-unit persistence is a stated differentiator ([[Ideas Inbox]]) and the emotional core of the campaign. McCullough's warning is that unbounded army persistence *"grows out of hand really quickly"* — which is exactly why our [[Progression]] layer needs a valve. **His stated answer in the core book is to remove army persistence entirely; ours has to be a cap or a tax** (see [[Trench Crusade#Cap the veterans, don't tax them]]). Recording the warning, not adopting the fix.

### Two years later, he broke his own rule — on purpose

The core book's answer was "don't persist units." *Battlesworn* (the first supplement, 2020 same year onward) walks that back with an **optional** Battle Honours system that does exactly the thing the core book warned against — and does it with a specific, load-bearing set of guardrails that directly answers our veteran price-vs-cap fork. Full mechanism at [[Oathmark#Two prices for one veteran, spent not stored]]. The short version: it's a **cap and a tax simultaneously** (max 3 honours, +10% compounding cost each), the "power" it buys is a **spend-not-store reroll currency** that refreshes every battle rather than a permanent stat bump, and destruction is usually **survivable** (an 8-in-10 chance of recovering, only 2-in-10 of permanent loss). It is the single most transferable mechanism in this whole capture for [[Progression]] and [[Damage]].

## Concentric rings and the rarity gate

**Type:** Faction · **Take:** ⚙️ adapt

The Kingdom Sheet is **five concentric circles** — explicitly *"not a map"* but *"a visual guide based on how far certain territories are from the seat of power."* Region 1 holds 1 territory (your capital), Region 2 holds 2, Region 3 holds 3, Region 4 holds 4 — **10 territories at creation** — and **Region 5 starts empty, reserved for conquest.**

There is **no cost and no currency in kingdom building.** There are slots plus a rarity gate that tightens toward the throne **[FACT]**:

| Region | Territories | From your capital's own race list | From any *other* race's list |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | your capital city (rarity 1) | — |
| 2 | 2 | rarity ≤ **2** | rarity ≤ **1** |
| 3 | 3 | rarity ≤ **3** | rarity ≤ **2** |
| 4 | 4 | **anything, no limitations** | **anything, no limitations** |
| 5 | 0 at start | conquest only | conquest only |

**Why it works — concentric permissiveness.** Your heartland is racially coherent *by construction*; your frontier is a free-for-all. **It produces thematic armies without a single "you may not take X" rule.** One table of rarity numbers replaces an entire chapter of faction restrictions.

**For Settlements.** [[Territory]] and [[Factions]]. A rarity number per territory type, tightening toward the settlement core, would give us thematic crews with no prohibition rules to write, argue about, or errata.

**Confirmed against the full core rulebook, plus one more layer.** The table above is verified verbatim against the rulebook's own worked example (p.78): *"if your capital is a Dwarf City, you can choose any of the terrain types with a rarity of (2) on the Dwarf Terrain List… or any of the terrain types with a rarity of (1) from the Human Terrain, Elf Terrain, or Goblin and Orc Terrain Lists."* **[FACT, confirmed]** There is also a fifth, race-agnostic list sitting outside the gate entirely: the **Unaligned Terrain List** (Ancient Ruins, Glade, High Fells, Caverns — all rarity 3, granting Gargoyles, a Bodach, a Dragon, Surmas) is available to *any* capital on equal footing, no matter which race it belongs to **[FACT, p.81]**. It's a fifth, monster-flavoured tier layered on top of the four racial rarity ladders — worth the same treatment for a settlement's own "neutral/wilderness" territory options.

## Occupied, never destroyed

**Type:** Settlement · **Take:** ⚙️ adapt

Territory losses are **soft and reversible** — a territory is occupied, not razed. A bad run costs you access, not existence, and the campaign remains recoverable.

**For Settlements.** Relevant to raid outcomes in [[Settlement]] and [[Campaign]]. A raid that permanently destroys a structure is a campaign-ending event for the loser; a raid that **occupies** or **disables** it for N turns is the same drama with a floor under it.

**The full campaign chapter confirms it and adds the precise mechanism — occupation taxes reliability, it doesn't ban access [FACT, p.155]:**

> *"Any figures you draw from an enemy-occupied territory to include in your army suffer a +1 penalty to their Activation stat."*

Oathmark's Activation stat is rolled-under (**lower is better**), so +1 is a real, felt cost, not a cosmetic one — but it is a **penalty, never a prohibition**. You can still field the Ogres from your occupied Rough Hills; they just activate less reliably. **This is the load-bearing distinction: "occupied" degrades quality, it never removes legality.** A direct, ready-made answer for how a Settlements raid should hit an *unoccupied structure's output* without making the structure itself illegal to use.

**Six raid types, six different stakes — not one generic "raid."** Each campaign turn's battle type is rolled (d10, weighted toward the middle) and each type answers a different question about what a win is worth **[FACT, pp.152-157]**:

| Roll | Battle type | What the winner gets |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Deep Strike | Occupy any territory except the capital (unless it's the only one left unoccupied) |
| 2–3 | Invasion | Occupy any territory with an open or an occupied-adjacent border |
| 4–5 | Border Strike | Occupy any territory with an open border (falls back to Invasion if none exist) |
| 6–7 | Border Clash | Occupy one open-border territory, or an occupied-border one if no open borders remain |
| 8–9 | Territorial Dispute | Add a **new** territory to an open Region 5 slot (or swap one, if Region 5 is full) |
| 10 | Punitive Expedition | An entire *region* (not just one territory) counts occupied for **one turn only**, then auto-reverts |

The loser, in every case except Territorial Dispute, may instead **free one already-occupied territory** — so a losing streak has a built-in path back to par, not just a floor. **Punitive Expedition is the standout mechanism**: a bigger, scarier consequence (a whole region, not one tile) that is *automatically temporary* — no bookkeeping, no "remember to free this eventually," it just expires. And the hard loss condition sits right where you'd expect it: **[FACT, p.157]** *"If your capital city is occupied at the end of a campaign turn, your kingdom falls and you lose the campaign."* Soft, reversible loss everywhere else; one specific, named tile where loss is final.

**Growth has a hard ceiling too, and it's rarely mentioned alongside the "menu" finding.** Territorial Dispute (and every other "add a territory" reward across the core book and both supplements) only *adds* a territory while Region 5 has an "open slot"; every one of them explicitly falls back to "replace a territory already in Region 5 instead" the moment it's full **[FACT — worded identically across all three books]**. Region 5 is therefore a **finite** space — Regions 1–4 are fixed forever at creation (1+2+3+4 = 10 territories) and Region 5 is the *only* place the kingdom can ever grow. **The exact number of Region 5 slots is never stated as a number anywhere in the text of any of the three books** — it's presumably shown as a fixed count of circle-segments on the physical Kingdom Sheet image, which none of this capture's text extraction can read **[NOT FOUND]**. What the rules text guarantees regardless of the exact count: once Region 5 fills, every further "win" of this type stops being *growth* and becomes *reshuffling* — swap one territory for a better one, but the kingdom's total size is capped for the rest of the campaign. Worth naming alongside "widens the menu, never the budget" as the other half of the same anti-snowball design: **the menu itself has a hard back cover, even if we don't know its exact page count.**

## Four more mechanisms worth taking

**[FACT — originally from the Army Planner and Kingdom extract; re-verified line-by-line against the full core rulebook this pass]**

1. **Two unlock grammars per territory.** Each of the **39 terrain types** (Dwarf 8, Human 9, Elf 9, Goblin & Orc 9, Unaligned 4 — recounted and confirmed exactly against the core rulebook's Terrain Lists, pp.78–81) either unlocks a unit **uncapped** (*"you may purchase as many orc and goblin slaves as you can afford"*) or with a numeric cap printed before the figure's name. One column, two behaviours.
2. **Caps that reward diversity.** The 4-unit limit doesn't carry across races — **so diversity is the only legal route to a wider army.** Confirmed verbatim, p.83: *"this does not carry across races within an army, so your force may have four units of human soldiers and four units of elf soldiers without breaking the four unit limit."*
3. **Character access is gated by territory, not by a stated "buildings cap heroes" formula.** ~~Heroes capped by buildings, not by a points percentage~~ — **correcting the earlier extract-only read.** The only *explicit numeric* character cap found anywhere in the core rulebook is *"no army may contain more than one general"* (repeated on every race's Terrain List). Kings, Princes, Champions, and Spellcasters are gated the same way any other unit is: by owning the terrain that unlocks them, and by that terrain's own rarity/region restrictions. Since city terrain (the only source of Kings/Princes) is capped at exactly one per kingdom by definition — you have one capital — and non-city hero-granting terrain (Hermitages, Towers, Monastery, etc.) competes for the same finite territory slots (10 fixed at creation, plus whatever Region 5 permits — see [[Oathmark#Occupied, never destroyed]]) as every other terrain type, heroes end up scarce *by construction*, not by an explicit percentage or headcount rule. **The mechanism is still transferable** — [[Structures]] gating access to Specialist/Leader unlocks via territory scarcity, rather than a % cap, is more thematic and harder to game — but the earlier note overstated it as an explicit formula that isn't actually printed.
4. **Growth is a menu, so the menu has to be worth reading.** Lateral growth only feels like progress if the new options are genuinely different, not just numerically better — which puts the design load on [[Weapons]] and [[Skill Paths]] breadth.

## Two prices for one veteran, spent not stored

**Type:** Campaign · **Take:** ⭐ steal — the strongest single data point yet on our veteran price-vs-cap fork

*Battlesworn*'s **Battle Honours** system is the core book's "armies don't persist" rule broken deliberately, two years later, as an optional add-on — and it answers our own fork ([[Trench Crusade#Cap the veterans, don't tax them]] vs [[The Walking Dead All Out War#Re-pricing veterans]] vs [[Spectre Operations#Veteran Pricing as a Flat Surcharge, Never a Cap]]) by **refusing to pick a side**. It does all three things other games do separately, at once **[FACT — Battlesworn pp.7-17]**:

- **A hard cap.** *"No unit may have more than three battle honours."* A veteran plateaus; it cannot become a monster.
- **A compounding tax, exactly like Trench Crusade's opposite number.** Each honour adds **+10% of current cost, rounding to nearest point, compounding on prior honours** — a 418-point unit reaches 460, then 506, then 557 points across its three honours. Unlike Trench Crusade (which explicitly refuses to re-price), Oathmark re-prices *and* caps in the same system.
- **What the price buys is a spent resource, not a permanent stat.** A battle honour is a **one-use reroll token** for an activation, morale, combat, or shooting die — never a Champion Die — one honour per roll, and *the full set refreshes at the start of every battle the unit survives, whether spent or not* — *"a unit cannot save battle honours from one battle to the next."* This is the detail the other three data points don't have: **the veteran's power is entirely tactical flexibility on demand, never a compounding numerical edge outside that die roll.** No amount of veteran units can out-scale the game's own math, because the "power" never leaves the reroll layer.
- **A d10 injury table, weighted to forgive.** A unit reduced to zero figures rolls: **1–2 destroyed permanently** (20%), **3–4 heavy casualties** — sits out `1D6÷2` rounded up battles (20%), **5–10 light casualties** — returns at full strength next game (60%). Wiping a veteran unit is a real risk, but permanent loss is the minority outcome, not the norm.
- **An explicit, named bookkeeping valve.** Only **one** unit with battle honours may be fielded per army by default — not because the rules require it, but because *"this is merely to keep the game simple… once players are comfortable… they may decide to try a game in which several units per side have battle honours."* The designer caps *simultaneous tracked complexity per game*, not the *total pool a kingdom may accumulate* — a genuinely different lever from anything else in this vault's veteran-fork data.

**Why it works.** It sidesteps the price-vs-cap either/or by converting "veteran power" into a currency that can't compound: a reroll spent this battle doesn't exist next battle. Stat inflation is structurally impossible because there's no permanent stat being inflated.

**For Settlements.** Directly answers the open question on [[Progression]] and [[Damage]] for **per-unit persistence and injury**. A named crew member could carry 1–3 "battle honour"-style tokens (reroll a failed roll once per use, refreshed each mission), cost a compounding tax capped at three tiers, and roll on a weighted table on defeat that mostly forgives and rarely kills permanently. This is closer to what Settlements' Stress/wound system already values (a spendable resource beats a flat stat bump) than any of the price-only or cap-only precedents in this vault. The one thing to watch: Oathmark's "only one veteran fielded per army" is a **crew-size-independent** simplification that matters less for us, since Settlements crews are already small — we can likely afford *several* honoured operators per crew without the bookkeeping load that worried McCullough at mass-battle scale.

## Reuse before purchase: formations before new figures

**Type:** Settlement · **Take:** ⭐ steal

*Bane of Kings*' **Special Formations** chapter unlocks new *tactical modes for units already in your list* — Phalanx and Square for spearmen, Skirmish formation to let small archer groups screen infantry instead of hiding behind it — gated by owning the matching terrain, exactly like any other unlock **[FACT, pp.7-8]**. The designer states the goal outright:

> *"The goal of these rules is to give players new ways to play and new strategies to try **without having to buy or paint new units**!"*

Mechanically it's gated the same way units are — a territory grants access to a formation for up to 4 units, no army may field more than 4 units with the same formation — so it costs the designer nothing new to write (same slot-and-cap grammar) while giving the player a reason to want the territory that has nothing to do with raw unit access.

**Why it works.** It's a second, cheaper channel for "the menu widens" that doesn't require designing a single new figure, stat line, or price. Growth can be **a new verb**, not just a new noun.

**For Settlements.** A structure or territory tier could unlock a new *use* for existing gear or a squad you already own — a formation, a fire mode, a coordinated action — rather than only new [[Weapons]] or [[Structures]]. Cheaper to design, cheaper to balance (it reuses an existing unit's cost), and it stretches the "worth reading" menu from point 4 above without inflating the catalogue of purchasable things. See [[Skill Paths]] and [[Structures]].

## Unique territories: a fixed, contested set of trophies

**Type:** Settlement · **Take:** ⚙️ adapt

Both supplements add **Unique Territories** — named, one-of-a-kind additions (Abandoned Temple Complex, Island of Flowers, Gold Mine, The High Seat, Prison of the Ice Queen) that are won only from a specific scenario or **taken from a rival kingdom that already has one [FACT, Battlesworn p.70; Bane of Kings pp.75-76]**:

- **Only one kingdom in a given campaign may ever hold a given unique territory at a time.** It's a fixed-supply trophy, not a printed unlock anyone can independently earn.
- **Taking one is a two-step process, not a single win.** You must first *occupy* the territory (during which neither side gets its benefit), then win a **second** battle against the same kingdom to actually *claim* it — converting it from occupied to fully transferred, on the loser's Kingdom Sheet to the winner's.
- **Effects are qualitative, never "+more army."** A spellcaster gets one extra known spell (Abandoned Temple Complex); reroll the destroyed-unit table and pick the better result (Island of Flowers); one unit gets Courage (1) for a game (Gold Mine); one unit gets +1 to morale tests (The High Seat). Every single one is a **once-per-game or passive qualitative edge**, none is a stat that compounds or a bigger points allowance — the "menu, not budget" rule holds even at the rarest tier of content in the game.
- **Prison of the Ice Queen is structurally different and worth flagging on its own:** it doesn't take a territory slot at all — it's added *on top of* an existing Region 4 or 5 territory (*"two territories occupy one spot"*), granting access to an alternate, thematic sub-table (Winter Strange Events) without displacing anything.

**Why it works.** A small, fixed pool of named trophies gives players something to specifically fight over besides raw territory count — and because the pool never grows, it can't be power-crept; only *possession* changes hands.

**For Settlements.** A handful of named, one-per-campaign structures or locations — not bought, only captured, each with a small qualitative (never budget-scaling) perk — would give raids a target worth planning around beyond "hit whatever's biggest." Relevant to [[Territory]] and [[Scenarios]]. The two-step occupy-then-claim process is also a clean, ready-made template for *"you can't steal it in one raid, but you can set up the steal."*

## Kingdom Events, and the one crack in "growth never costs budget"

**Type:** Campaign · **Take:** ⚙️ adapt

*Bane of Kings* adds a between-battle random event system, rolled **after** each battle (not before, so players have time to react before the next game) **[FACT, pp.28-29]**: roll 1d10 on the Kingdom Event Table — a 1–6 sends you to one of six themed sub-tables (Political / Internal / External / Natural / Magical / Miscellaneous), a **7–10 (40%) is explicitly "No Event"** with an optional single reroll. A second, entirely separate **Passage of Time** table is offered purely for narrative colour — years-between-events for a kingdom "Chronicle" — and the designer is explicit that *"this whole section is just for fun, so do not take it too seriously,"* deliberately decoupling narrative pacing from any mechanical effect.

**The one documented exception to "growth never costs budget" lives inside this chapter.** The **Land Purchase** political event lets a kingdom buy a new Region 5 territory **outside of combat** — but only at a price **[FACT, p.35]**:

> *"You may choose to immediately add any territory you wish to Region 5 on your Kingdom Sheet… However, if you do so, you receive 10% fewer points to build your army for the next battle."*

Every other route to growth in all three books (winning a battle, a Territorial Dispute, a Military Expedition reward) is free at the point of use. The **one** time growth is priced, it's specifically the case where the territory was never earned through the game's actual currency — a battle. Read generously, this is the designer using a tax exactly once, and precisely where he needed a reason to *not* make growth completely free of any cost at all.

**For Settlements.** The event-table shape (mostly nothing, occasionally a themed complication, purely narrative time-tracking kept separate from mechanics) is a light, cheap way to give a [[Campaign]] texture between missions without new subsystems. More importantly: if Settlements ever wants a "buy a Structure/territory outside of a raid" path, Oathmark's own precedent says **that's exactly the path to tax** — earned (combat) growth stays free, purchased-outside-the-fiction growth is the one lever that costs Crew Rating. See [[Campaign]] and [[Economy]].
