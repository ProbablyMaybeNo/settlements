SETTLEMENTS POINTS REBUILD - EVIDENCE AND EXPLANATIONS
Plain prose, hard-wrapped at 76 characters, no tables, no box drawing.

THE ONE-LINE TEST THIS WHOLE AUDIT KEEPS APPLYING

  A number that looks precise, carries a tag or a confidence interval, and
  is denominated in something nobody has measured.

That is what every finding in this rebuild has turned out to be an instance
of. Apply it to anything before trusting it. A confidence interval bounds
sampling noise; it says nothing about whether the units mean anything.

1. WHY BALANCED_GOALS IS NOT A CLEAN FIX, AND WHY IT CANNOT BE MADE ONE

The setup. The Take-a-Hold board has three objectives on the centreline. At
spawn the engine spreads each crew evenly across its deployment band and
gives every model a goal objective. The default rule is "each model goes to
whichever objective is nearest to it". The BALANCED_GOALS flag replaces that
with a round robin, intended to stop objective coverage depending on crew
size. It is off by default.

What BALANCED_GOALS actually does. Each model sorts the three objectives by
distance from itself and takes the one at index i modulo three, where i is
that model's own position in the crew list.

Why that does not work. The round robin index is applied to a list sorted
separately for each model, and models sit at different places on the board,
so their sorted orders differ. The leftmost model's nearest objective is the
left one and index zero picks it. The middle model's nearest is the centre,
so index one picks its second nearest, which is the left one. The rightmost
model's nearest is the right objective, so index two picks its third
nearest, which is again the left one. Each model believes it is claiming a
different slot, but they are indexing into differently ordered lists, so the
picks collide instead of spreading.

Measured, with the flag on, a three-model crew sends all three models to the
same objective, a distribution of three, zero, zero, leaving the other two
objectives uncontested. A four-model crew comes out three, zero, one.

Now the part that changes the conclusion. I measured what the default rule
actually produces at every crew size from two to twelve, and the default is
already optimally balanced at every one of them. The gap between the
busiest and emptiest objective never exceeds one model, which is the best
any assignment can do when the model count is not divisible by three. Two
models split one, zero, one. Three split one, one, one. Four split one, two,
one. Five split two, one, two. Six split two, two, two. Twelve split four,
four, four. Every size, spread of at most one.

Against that, BALANCED_GOALS is never better and frequently much worse. Its
spread is three at three models and at four models, two at six, nine, ten
and twelve, and merely equal to the default at two, five, seven, eight and
eleven. There is not a single crew size where it improves on the rule it
replaces.

So the flag cannot be repaired to satisfy the invariant "never worse than
naive at any crew size", because the quantity it optimises is already
optimal under naive. Count balance was never the problem. The correct action
is to delete the flag, not to fix it and not to gate it behind a crew-size
threshold. A threshold would be worse than either branch: it would hide a
known-broken algorithm behind a cutoff and guarantee that whichever sizes
sit near that cutoff become the next unexplained artefact.

That leaves the real question open, which is the honest report. The bug note
that introduced BALANCED_GOALS was responding to something genuine: win rate
on Take-a-Hold is not monotonic in crew size. That observation is correct.
Its stated cause is not.

What is actually happening. I measured crew sizes two through nine against a
fixed six-model crew, eight hundred games per cell, sides swapped, using
uniform rifle crews. On Take-a-Hold, every even-sized crew drew one hundred
percent of its games and both sides scored exactly zero victory points. Odd
sizes scored between roughly half a point and one point, and the six-model
opponent scored zero in every single row. The scenario's own ceiling is
fifteen victory points. So these crews are scoring nothing at all, and the
scenario is resolving almost entirely by draw.

The mechanism is mutual contest. Holding an objective requires a standing
friendly within three inches and no enemy within three inches. With
mirror-symmetric deployment and both crews sending models to the same
objectives, every objective is contested every round, so nobody holds and
nobody scores. When the two crews have different shapes, an objective is
occasionally left uncontested and somebody scores a single point. That
difference in shape, not any difference in strength, is what produces the
apparent non-monotonicity.

The same comparison on Annihilate behaves the way you would expect. Draws
fall from ninety-three percent at two models to thirteen percent at nine,
victory points rise steadily from zero to one point three, and the win count
climbs from two to five hundred and ninety-four. There is one residual
inversion at three models versus four, so it is not perfectly clean, but the
scenario resolves and crew size does what crew size should do.

What this means for the rebuild. The bare-body atom, meaning what one
additional model is worth, cannot be measured on Take-a-Hold with pure
ranged crews under either flag setting, because the scenario does not score.
It is measurable on Annihilate, with the residual three-versus-four
inversion stated as a caveat. Any historical finding that compares crews of
different sizes on Take-a-Hold is measuring draw behaviour rather than
strength, and that includes the model-count regression cited as the evidence
closing the additive-versus-multiplicative pricing question.

Correction to the first version of this section. It claimed the default
rule's coverage "oscillates with crew size", offering the one, two, one
split at four models and the two, one, two split at five as examples of
lopsidedness. Both are in fact optimally balanced, and I had repeated a
characterisation from the earlier analysis pass without measuring it. The
real defect is in the scenario's scoring, not in the goal assignment.


2. WHAT ARMOUR-LEVEL PRICING IS BLOCKED ON

First, what is not in dispute. Armour subtracts from the injury roll, and
the injury roll is one ten-sided die plus damage minus armour against a
target of seven. Each point of armour is therefore a flat ten percent
reduction in the chance of being wounded, which means minus two is exactly
twice as valuable as minus one, with no diminishing return inside the game's
own caps. Both simulators also agree that a point of armour is worth roughly
the same as a point of damage. So the shape of the armour ladder is settled
and the ratio between armour and damage is settled.

What is in dispute is the level, meaning how many Credits one point of
armour should cost. Two methods answer that, and they disagree by a factor
of four.

The first prices armour against the damage atom. Armour measures at about
one times damage per point, damage is pegged at fifteen Credits, so armour
lands near fifteen Credits per point. This is a clean relative measurement
and it is not the thing that is blocked.

The second is rebuild-to-pay. You give a crew armour, then force it to pay
by dropping something else until its win rate returns to baseline, and read
the price off what it had to give up. This breaks even nearer sixty Credits
per point.

The second method is the one a catalogue price actually asks for. A price is
not "how much win rate does this deliver in isolation", it is "how much of a
fixed budget should this consume", which is a statement about opportunity
cost inside a real list.

Here is the block. Rebuild-to-pay measures armour's price in units of
whatever you gave up, and in practice you give up a body or a weapon.
Converting that to Credits requires knowing what the dropped thing is worth.
The weapon class prices are not measurements. The costing engine says so
itself: the block of weapon class costs is introduced by a comment reading
"Weapon classes, legacy times ten", meaning they were carried across from an
older, much smaller point scale by multiplying by ten. Standard Ranged sits
at one hundred Credits on that basis and nothing has ever measured it.

So rebuild-to-pay today yields "armour costs about as much as a rifle",
which is a true relative statement, and then converts it using a rifle price
that is itself unmeasured. The armour number inherits that error silently.
You cannot compare a measured defensive atom against an inherited offensive
class price and get a meaningful ratio, which is exactly what the project's
own points documentation concluded when it called this a body-versus-damage
scale problem rather than an armour problem.

A second complication constrains the ordering. Armour and payloads are
jointly determined. A payload only lands on a hit that fails to wound, so
more armour on the table means payloads land more often and every payload
becomes more valuable. In the other direction, Armour Piercing is worth a
full damage step against an armoured target and nothing against a bare one,
so its price depends on how many models wear armour, which depends on what
armour costs. Three prices, each moving the other two.

A provenance problem worth stating plainly. The armour numbers in the
costing engine, Light at thirty and Heavy at sixty, are tagged in the source
as measured, citing a file called balance/armourprice.py. That file does not
exist. It is not in the working tree and the entire history across all
branches contains zero commits that have ever touched a file by that name.
The number driving the engine has no recoverable derivation, so it must be
measured from scratch with no prior, not treated as a rough starting guess.

Separately, the ruled price in the master rules note is Light sixty and
Heavy one hundred, which is not a clean doubling. Since each armour point is
a flat ten percent on the injury roll, linearity is a property of the
arithmetic rather than a stylistic choice, so a ruled price that breaks it
encodes an unmeasured diminishing-return judgment. The ruling on this is
that the measured invariant wins and Heavy is exactly twice Light once the
level is settled.

So the order is forced. Measure the weapon classes from their own atoms
first, so Standard Ranged means something. Then run armour rebuild-to-pay
against a catalogue whose prices are real. Then re-run the payload table,
because payload values move with how much armour is actually worn once
armour is priced correctly. Doing armour first would produce a number that
looks precise, carries a confidence interval, and is denominated in a
currency of unknown value.
